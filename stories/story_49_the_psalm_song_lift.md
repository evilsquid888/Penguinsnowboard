# Story 49: The Psalm-Song Lift
### *Inspired by Psalms*
**Characters:** Kingfisher Choir, Barnaby Beaksworth  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Lift Line Ridge  
**Theme:** Praise in every mood

---

## Opening Wink

If moonbeams had mittens, this would be the night they came out to clap for the valley.

## The Story

The Chairlift Singalong was **Barnaby Beaksworth's** idea, which meant it was simultaneously wonderful and slightly chaotic from the very beginning.

The concept was simple: as riders rode the chairlift up Lift Line Ridge, the Kingfisher Choir — seven brightly colored kingfishers who lived in the valley's spruce grove and had very strong opinions about harmony — would be stationed at intervals along the lift line, each perched on a wooden platform installed specially for the event. As your chair passed each platform, the kingfisher there would start a different song, and you were encouraged — not required, *encouraged* — to sing along.

"It will be a moving musical experience," said Barnaby. "Literally moving. Because of the chairs."

"What if people don't want to sing?" said Nora.

"Then they hum," said Barnaby. "Or they listen. The point is the music meets you wherever you are."

---

The first rider up the lift on the morning of the Singalong was **Magnus Meltsworth**, the large polar bear, and he was in a mood.

Not a bad mood exactly — more of a grey mood. The kind that sits on your shoulders like wet snow. He'd been inside for three days during the storm, he'd run out of reading material, and the cabin had felt very small.

He settled into his chair. The lift began to move.

At the first platform, **Twill** the kingfisher — bright cerulean blue, tiny, with an expression of extreme professionalism — opened her beak and sang something in a minor key. Low and patient, like a river under ice. A song for the grey days.

Magnus's eyebrows, which had been doing their furrowed thing, shifted slightly.

He did not sing. But he listened.

At the second platform, **Crispin** was singing something completely different — quick and bright and silly, with a verse about a goose who fell into a snowbank and came out wearing the snowbank as a hat. Three riders in the chairs ahead of Magnus were already laughing.

At the third platform, **Melodia** sang something that sounded like the feeling of the first warm day in spring — hopeful and reaching upward, the musical equivalent of a crocus pushing through frozen ground.

By the fourth platform, Magnus had stopped frowning.

By the fifth, he was humming. Quietly, to himself, barely audible. But humming.

---

The chaos started — predictably — with **Finnegan Flipsworth**.

Finnegan arrived at the lift line bursting with energy, as Finnegan always did, and he was loaded for sound: he had learned all seven of the Kingfisher Choir's songs in advance (he'd asked them to teach him, and they had, regretfully, agreed) and he was planning to sing every single one simultaneously, or at least as simultaneously as one creature could manage.

"I'll blend them," he explained to his chairlift seatmate, an elderly narwhal named Beryl who had simply wanted a quiet ride.

"That's not how harmony works," said Beryl.

"I've reinvented harmony," said Finnegan.

What happened next was that Finnegan, upon passing the first platform, began singing Twill's minor-key song but also trying to remember the silly goose verse from Crispin's song at the same time, which resulted in a minor-key song about a goose falling into a snowbank in a way that seemed somehow both sad and hilarious.

"That's actually good," said Beryl, despite herself.

Finnegan immediately fell off his side of the chair trying to bow.

The chair lurched. The safety bar held. Beryl held the safety bar with both flippers.

"SORRY, BERYL. I'M FINE. THE MUSIC LIVES ON."

---

Midway through the morning, **Barnaby** rode up himself.

He had been too busy organizing to take a turn earlier, and now he sat in the slowly moving chair with his feet dangling and his ears open, and the valley spread below him in its full winter magnificence — white bowls and dark pines and the smoke from the Warming Hut and the ribbon of the lift line climbing toward the ridge — and the Kingfisher Choir's songs came to him one by one as he moved through them.

The grey song. The silly song. The hopeful song. A fourth one he hadn't heard yet, which turned out to be almost wordless, just pure sound, the musical shape of gratitude. A fifth that was quick and brave. A sixth that was tender. And at the very top, where **Sable** the oldest kingfisher sat on the highest platform, a song that seemed to hold all the others inside it somehow — the grey and the silly and the hopeful and the grateful — all at once.

Barnaby got off at the top and stood in the snow for a moment.

A praise, he thought, for every mood. Not just the happy ones. All of them. The grey ones too. The silly ones especially.

He thought of Magnus humming on the lift.

He thought of Finnegan nearly falling off the chair.

He thought of Beryl, who had come for a quiet ride and gotten quite a different experience and had nonetheless been seen humming the goose verse on her second trip up.

A song for wherever you are.

That, thought Barnaby, was the whole point.

He had his cocoa thermos ready. He poured a cup, watched the chairlift carry creatures up through the music, and felt — quietly, simply — that this had been exactly the right idea.

---

"Slide into it!" shouted someone from the crowd, and that was all the invitation anybody needed.

But just as the event began, a surprise problem showed up. A marker flag vanished, a route felt confusing, and Finnegan announced three different plans at once. The valley got noisy in the way only excited animals can—half cheering, half panicking, and fully dramatic.

That was when the old story came to mind: **Psalms**.

Instead of forcing a quick fix, the crew slowed down and chose the heart of the story. They listened. They shared. They told the truth. They helped the smallest riders first. Bjorn steadied the line, Willa checked details, Barnaby passed emergency fish crackers, and Cleo spotted beauty even in the mess.

Soon the mood changed. What felt like chaos became teamwork. Skis carved clean arcs, snowboards sprayed sparkling powder, and even the seals kept rhythm with their flippers like tiny Olympic judges.

By sundown, the challenge was solved and everyone learned the same cozy lesson: **Praise in every mood**.

Under the aurora, the friends sat in a circle of warm lantern light. Somebody yawned. Somebody else yawned louder. Then they all laughed, tucked scarves around their necks, and promised to keep practicing kindness on and off the slopes.

"Tomorrow," said Barnaby, lifting a cocoa mug, "we do it again—only with fewer accidental faceplants."

No one promised that part.
