# Story 58: The Stargazer Snow Kings
### *Inspired by Wise Men Follow the Star — Matthew 2*
**Characters:** Magi Marmots, Captain Prism  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Star Dune  
**Theme:** Seeking light

---

## Opening Wink

If you ever see a star that seems brighter than all the others, and it looks like it is waiting for you -- well, maybe it is.

## The Story

Three travelers arrived at the eastern edge of Frostpeak Valley on the coldest morning anyone could remember, and they caused quite a stir.

The first was a great marmot named Caspar, who wore a fur-lined cape the color of cranberries and carried a wooden chest strapped to a sled behind him. The second was a marmot named Melchior, taller and thinner, dressed in robes of deep midnight blue, with a brass telescope slung over one shoulder. The third was Balthasar, the oldest of the group, wrapped in a cloak of forest green with golden stitching, and he rode the most magnificent pair of cross-country skis anyone in the valley had ever seen -- hand-carved, polished to a mirror shine, with tiny stars inlaid along the edges.

Piper Paddlefoot spotted them first from her perch on the halfpipe rim. She nearly fell off her snowboard.

"Who in the WORLD are those fancy marmots?" she shouted.

"They look important," said Cleo Coldwater, already reaching for her sketchbook. "Look at those cloaks. And that telescope! I want to paint that telescope."

The three marmots made their way to Herschel the Walrus's cocoa stand, which was really the social center of the entire valley. Herschel took one look at them -- the cloaks, the chests, the skis -- and poured three cups of his finest reserve cocoa without being asked.

"Travelers from far off," he said, his great whiskers curling with interest. "What brings you through the mountain passes in weather like this?"

Caspar wrapped his paws around the warm mug and sighed with relief. "We're following a star."

Herschel raised one bushy eyebrow. "A star."

"A very specific star," said Melchior, pulling out his telescope and polishing it on his sleeve. "I've been studying the night sky for thirty-seven years, and three weeks ago, a new star appeared. Brighter than anything in my charts. Brighter than the North Star. Brighter than the light on top of the Frostpeak Grand Lodge, and that thing is practically a lighthouse."

"It moves," added Balthasar quietly. He had a voice like a cello -- deep and warm and slow. "Not like other stars. It drifts westward, night by night, as if it's leading us somewhere."

"Leading you where?" asked Willa Wobble, who had appeared at the cocoa stand in that silent, magical way she had, like a small blue penguin made of mist.

The three marmots looked at each other.

"We don't exactly know," admitted Caspar. "But we believe -- we *feel* -- that wherever it stops, we'll find something extraordinary. Something worth the journey. Something worth the gifts we've brought."

"Gifts?" Barnaby Beaksworth leaned in, his eyes wide. Barnaby loved gifts almost as much as he loved fish crackers, and he loved fish crackers more than oxygen.

Caspar opened his chest just a crack. Inside, nestled in velvet, was a small golden snow globe -- not a cheap plastic one, but a real one, handblown glass filled with golden flakes that swirled and shimmered like captured sunlight. "From the craftsmen of Amber Ridge Valley," he said. "Gold, for someone worthy of honor."

Melchior opened his pack and produced a small cedar box. When he lifted the lid, the most extraordinary smell filled the air -- warm, smoky, sweet, like a forest after rain mixed with cinnamon and something ancient and holy. "Frankincense resin," he said. "From the old pine groves of Evergreen Deep. A gift for someone set apart."

Balthasar reached into his cloak and held up a tiny crystal bottle filled with amber liquid. "Myrrh oil," he said. "Pressed from winter herbs. Precious and rare. A gift for someone whose story will be remembered forever."

The valley went quiet.

"Those are serious gifts," said Bjorn Bigpaws, who had been listening from a respectful distance, his great arms folded. "Not trinkets. Not souvenirs. You really believe this star is leading you to someone that important?"

Balthasar looked at the polar bear with steady, wise eyes. "We've crossed three mountain ranges, one frozen lake, and a canyon that I don't want to talk about," he said. "Melchior's telescope froze to his face twice. Caspar's sled broke and we rebuilt it with birch branches and stubbornness. I lost feeling in my left foot somewhere around Icewall Pass and it hasn't come back yet." He smiled. "Yes. We believe."

Bjorn unfolded his arms and nodded slowly. "Then we'll help you find where that star leads."

That night, the whole valley turned out to watch. Melchior set up his telescope on Star Dune -- the highest open hilltop in Frostpeak -- and everyone took turns looking.

There it was. The star. Bigger and brighter than anything else in the sky, shining with a steady, warm, golden light that didn't twinkle or flicker. It hung low over the western ridge, pointing down toward the valley below, toward --

"That's the old stable," said Barnaby. "Behind the Grand Lodge. That's where the star is pointing."

"The stable?" Finnegan Flipsworth scratched his crest. "That drafty old place? Are you sure your telescope isn't on backwards?"

"Telescopes don't go backwards," said Melchior patiently.

"Mine would," said Finnegan.

The three marmots set off through the snow, and Piper insisted on leading the way. "Slide into it!" she shouted, carving a path down the hillside on her snowboard, sending up great plumes of powder that sparkled in the starlight. The marmots followed on skis and sled, and behind them came half the valley -- Bjorn and Nora Snowmane walking side by side, Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff tumbling through the snow like furry cannonballs, Willa padding along with her quiet steady steps, and Cleo sketching the whole procession as she walked, her pencil never stopping.

When they reached the stable, Caspar paused at the door. He looked at his companions. The star blazed directly overhead, so bright it cast shadows on the snow.

"This is it," he whispered.

They went in.

The stable was warm and humble and glowing. Mary Moose sat by the feeding trough, where a tiny baby slept in a nest of hay and soft wool. Jo stood beside her. A few penguins and a seal in a patrol vest were already there, looking like they'd arrived in a hurry and hadn't left since.

Caspar looked at the baby -- so small, so ordinary, so impossibly peaceful -- and his breath caught. Slowly, he knelt on the hay-covered floor and set the golden snow globe beside the trough. The golden flakes inside caught the lantern light and swirled, filling the stable with dancing sparks.

Melchior knelt too and placed the cedar box of frankincense beside the globe. The warm, holy smell of it rose like a prayer and mixed with the scent of pine and hay.

Balthasar was last. The old marmot's eyes were shining. He set the crystal bottle of myrrh down with trembling paws, and then he sat back and let out a long, deep breath, like he'd been holding it for the entire journey.

"Three mountain ranges," he said softly. "One frozen lake. A canyon. My left foot." He looked at the sleeping baby and smiled the deepest smile of his life. "Worth every single step."

They stayed for a long time. Herschel arrived with cocoa for everyone, because Herschel always arrived with cocoa. Barnaby handed out fish crackers. Cleo sat in the corner and filled three pages with sketches -- the gifts, the star through the window, the marmots' faces as they gazed at the child.

Later, as the travelers prepared to leave, Willa walked with Balthasar to the door.

"How will you get home?" she asked. "Back over those mountains?"

Balthasar looked up at the sky. The star still shone, but softer now, gentler, like a nightlight in a child's room. "We'll find a different way back," he said. "Sometimes the journey home is different from the journey there. You go looking for one thing and you find something that changes everything."

Willa nodded. "What did you find?"

The old marmot pulled his green cloak tighter and smiled. "That the biggest light in the universe," he said, "chose the smallest room."

And with that, the three stargazer snow kings set off into the night, their tracks in the snow slowly filling with fresh powder, until the only sign they had ever been there was the faint smell of frankincense and the golden glow still swirling in a tiny snow globe, next to a sleeping baby, in the humblest stable in Frostpeak Valley.
