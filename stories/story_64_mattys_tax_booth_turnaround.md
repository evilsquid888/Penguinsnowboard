## The Story

**Matty Mink** had run the checkpoint booth at Checkpoint Curve for seven years, and he was not, by most accounts, anyone's favorite creature in Frostpeak Valley.

The checkpoint was where all resort guests checked in and paid the access fee, and Matty ran it with the precise exactitude of someone who had learned long ago that any flexibility in the rules produced complaints and complications. He charged exactly what the fee schedule said. If you were a minute late for your guest pass window, the fee doubled. If your equipment tag wasn't readable, you went to the back of the line. If you tried to bring more guests than your pass allowed, Matty would count them, loudly, in front of everyone.

He had been told he was too strict. He had been called other things too, behind his back, by creatures who felt they'd been treated unfairly. He knew they said it. He'd stopped minding, mostly.

The thing was: nobody ever just talked to him. Not really. They talked at him — to complain, or to argue the fee, or to hurry him along so they could get to the slopes. In seven years at the checkpoint, almost nobody had asked him how he was or whether he'd had a good morning or what he thought about the snow conditions.

**Nora Snowmane** asked him all three, one Tuesday in February.

She was in no hurry. She had her pass ready, paid the fee without discussion, and then stood at the window and said: "How are you? You're always here. I pass this booth every week and I never ask."

Matty looked at her.

"I'm fine," he said. Then, because she was still looking at him with the expression of someone who actually wanted to know: "It's been a long season. The new fee schedule made things complicated. People are angry about the changes and I'm the one they're angry at."

"That seems hard," said Nora.

"It's the job."

"It doesn't have to be just the job," said Nora. "You're allowed to be more than the job."

Matty blinked.

Nobody had ever said that to him.

"I used to ski," he said, after a moment. "Before I worked the booth. I was actually — I was pretty good. I just needed the income and the booth paid steadily and then—" he shrugged "—seven years."

Nora looked at him thoughtfully. "The booth doesn't need to be staffed on Tuesdays between eleven and two. I checked the schedule. Aldric covers it."

"I know."

"The east face has excellent conditions right now."

A pause.

"Are you offering to—"

"I'm suggesting," said Nora carefully, "that the booth does not require you to give it every Tuesday."

She went through. Matty watched her go.

---

He skied the east face that Tuesday, between eleven and two.

He was rusty. He fell twice on the steeper section in ways he would not have fallen seven years ago. But the form came back in pieces — the weight shift on the turns, the way the board felt when it was right — and by the last run he was carving properly, the valley spread out below him and the cold air in his face and the whole mountain wide and white.

He was at the checkpoint the next morning. But something had shifted in how he sat behind the window. When a young family arrived flustered and two minutes late, he processed them at the regular rate without comment. When a rider's equipment tag was hard to read, he leaned forward and squinted and made it work rather than sending them to the back of the line.

Small things. But they accumulated.

By the end of the week, the Nestling Flock's Pip Starling had told Matty that his booth was her favorite part of arriving at the resort. "You always know the ice conditions," she said. "And you told me about the shortcut to the east face."

Matty had done that, yes. He had information nobody used — seven years of watching the mountain from the checkpoint — and sharing it took approximately fifteen extra seconds per person.

"Come find me at lunch Tuesday," said Nora, the following week. "I found a good spot for watching the midday light on the north ridge."

"I have the booth—"

"Aldric has the booth."

Matty was quiet for a moment. Then he pulled out his schedule and wrote himself a note.

Everyone can start fresh. Not just in dramatic moments, not just when life gives you a clear before-and-after. Sometimes it's Tuesday, between eleven and two, on the east face with rusty form and a wide mountain and the first run in seven years that felt like yourself.
