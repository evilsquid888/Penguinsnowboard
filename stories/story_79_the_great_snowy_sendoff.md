# Story 79: The Great Snowy Send-Off
### *Inspired by Great Commission — Matthew 28:18–20*
**Characters:** Comet Crane, Aurora Frostholm  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Trailhead Peak  
**Theme:** Share good news

---

## Opening Wink

Some nights the wind whistles; tonight it hummed like it knew a secret worth sharing.

## The Story

**Comet Crane** was leaving Frostpeak Valley at the end of the season, and nobody was quite ready.

She had been here for seven winters. In that time she had become the valley's most respected snowboard coach, the creature who had taught everyone from Willa Wobble's first carves to Finnegan's increasingly improbable but somehow successful trick variations to Pip Starling's junior competition prep. She knew the mountain the way a musician knows a beloved instrument — every run, every quirk, every condition that made it sing.

She was going north. There was a settlement there, newer than Frostpeak, with young riders and no coaches and snow conditions that everyone agreed were excellent but nobody was fully using. She had been asked to come. She had thought about it all season. She had said yes.

**Aurora Frostholm** had been the one who encouraged her to go.

"The valley can find another coach," Aurora had said. "That settlement can't find another you."

"I've been here seven winters," said Comet. "Everything I know is here."

"Everything you know is *in you*," Aurora corrected gently. "That's the point of knowing things. They go where you go."

---

The send-off at Trailhead Peak happened on the last Sunday before she left.

Not a small send-off. The whole valley came — because Comet was a person who had given seven winters to this place, and the valley knew how to honor that. There were lanterns on the trailhead posts. Barnaby had made a traveling pack of food for the journey. The Kingfisher Choir sang something warm and unhurried. The Nestling Flock, who had learned their rhythm-skating from Comet, gave her a joint handmade card with their footprints on it, which she folded very carefully and put in her jacket's inside pocket.

Comet stood at the trailhead and looked out at the assembled faces and felt the fullness of seven winters in her chest.

"Go everywhere," said Aurora.

"I'll come back," said Comet.

"Come back *and* go everywhere. Both."

Finnegan was sniffling. He was not pretending he wasn't. "COMET," he announced, loudly and emotionally, "YOU TAUGHT ME THE HALF-INVERTED THING AND I HAVE A LOT OF FEELINGS ABOUT THAT."

"I know," said Comet. "You're very good at it."

"THANK YOU."

"Finnegan."

"YES?"

"Stop doing it for fun on the beginner slopes. Safety."

"UNDERSTOOD. MOSTLY."

She skied the first section of the trailhead run one last time — not fast, not demonstratively, just clean and present, the way she always skied — and at the bottom of the first section she took the fork that led north, the one that went up and over the pass to the other side of the range.

The valley watched her go.

At the top of the pass she stopped and looked back for a moment — the whole bowl of Frostpeak spread below her, the dots of her friends at the trailhead, the lanterns just beginning to glow as the light shifted. She raised a wing. Probably too far to see. But she raised it anyway.

---

A week later, the first message arrived: a short note with a pressed juniper sprig, from the settlement up north. *Arrived safely. Eleven young riders who've never had a coach. First session tomorrow. Wish me luck.*

**Aurora** read it aloud at the Warming Hut.

"She didn't ask us to wish her luck," said Bjorn. "She said wish me luck."

"There's a difference?" said Pip.

"She asked," said Bjorn. "She let us be part of it."

He raised his cocoa mug. Everyone raised theirs.

"Good luck, Comet," said Aurora.

The valley drank cocoa and thought about the settlement up north, where eleven young riders were about to learn what good coaching looked like, which is to say what it looked like to be taught by someone who fully believed the good thing was worth passing on.

Share what you know. Go where you're needed. Come back if you can. The good news is the kind that doesn't run out when you give it away — it multiplies.
