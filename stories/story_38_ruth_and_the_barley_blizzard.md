# Story 38: Ruth and the Barley Blizzard
### *Inspired by Ruth — Ruth 1–4*
**Characters:** Ruthie Raccoon, Barnaby Beaksworth  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Harvest Hollow  
**Theme:** Loyal love

---

## Opening Wink

Sometimes the bravest thing in the whole wide winter isn't carving a perfect turn -- it's choosing to stay beside somebody when the trail gets rough.

## The Story

The blizzard arrived on a Tuesday, which was rude of it, because Tuesdays in Frostpeak Valley were supposed to be Barnaby Beaksworth's famous Fish-Cracker-and-Cocoa afternoons at Harvest Hollow. Instead, the wind howled like a walrus with a stubbed toe, and the snow fell so thick you couldn't see your own beak.

"Well," said Barnaby, pressing his chinstrap feathers flat against the gale, "that's the end of snack hour."

He was standing at the entrance to Harvest Hollow -- a wide, gentle slope where the valley's barley grass poked up through the snow in golden tufts every winter. Normally the animals gathered here to collect the frost-sweetened barley tips for Herschel the Walrus's famous barley-butter cocoa. But today, nobody was gathering anything. The supply shed had blown half-open, and barley bundles lay scattered across the slope like confetti after a very disorganized party.

That was when two figures appeared through the whiteout.

The first was old Nora Snowmane, the polar bear counselor, walking slowly with her big paws wrapped in extra scarves. She leaned on a carved walking stick, and her breath came in tired little clouds. Behind her, carrying two enormous packs, a suitcase, and what appeared to be a lamp made from a tin can, was a small raccoon nobody had ever seen before.

"Hello!" the raccoon called out, her voice bright even through the storm. "Is this Frostpeak Valley? We've been walking for three days!"

Barnaby blinked. "Three *days*? Through *this*?"

"Through worse, actually," said Nora, sinking onto a snow-dusted bench. "This is Ruthie. She's from Timber Creek, over the eastern ridge. The creek flooded and her whole village had to scatter. She chose to come with me."

"You didn't have to," Nora added quietly, looking at Ruthie.

Ruthie set down all the bags, adjusted her striped scarf, and said the thing she'd been saying for three days straight: "Where you go, I go. That's that."

Barnaby's beak wobbled. He was a penguin who cried at nice things, and this was extremely nice.

"Right," he sniffed. "Well. You both need cocoa immediately. Possibly soup. Definitely a blanket. Follow me."

He waddled them into the Harvest Hollow lodge, where Herschel the Walrus was stirring an enormous pot of peppermint broth with his tusks. Herschel looked up, studied the newcomers, and without a word ladled out two steaming bowls.

"Eat first," he rumbled. "Introductions after."

Ruthie ate three bowls. She hadn't had a hot meal since Timber Creek.

Over the next few days, the blizzard settled into a grumpy drizzle, and the real problem became clear: the barley supply was a mess. Half the harvest had blown across the slopes. The storage bins were jumbled. And the Frostpeak Winter Festival -- the big celebration where every animal got a mug of Herschel's legendary barley-butter cocoa -- was only ten days away.

"We'll never sort it in time," moaned Finnegan Flipsworth, flopping dramatically onto a snowbank. "It's over. The festival is ruined. I'm going to lie here forever."

"You've been lying there for forty-five seconds," said Willa Wobble.

"It feels like forever."

Ruthie stepped forward. "I can help. I'm good at gathering things. Back in Timber Creek, I sorted the whole autumn harvest by myself -- by color, by size, by crunchiness."

Piper Paddlefoot tilted her head. "By *crunchiness*?"

"You'd be surprised how useful that is."

So the next morning, before the sun had even painted the peaks pink, Ruthie was out on the slopes with snowshoes strapped to her small raccoon feet, a woven basket on her back, and her tin-can lantern swinging from her belt. She worked the edges of the fields -- the spots the main harvesters had missed, the corners where barley tufts hid under ice crusts and behind rocks. She didn't take from anyone else's pile. She only gleaned what had been left behind.

Barnaby watched from the lodge window, a fish cracker frozen halfway to his beak.

"She's been out there since dawn," he said.

"She was out there *before* dawn," corrected Willa, who noticed everything.

By noon, Ruthie had filled four baskets. She carried them into the sorting barn and organized every tuft by length, by freshness, by how much frost sugar still clung to the tips. Cleo Coldwater wandered in, saw the neat rows, and gasped.

"It's like art," Cleo whispered. "Barley art."

"It's just sorting," said Ruthie, but she smiled.

Word spread. Bjorn Bigpaws, the dignified polar bear leader, came to see the work for himself. He stood in the barn doorway, his shadow filling the whole frame, and said nothing for a long time.

"You did all this?" he finally asked.

"The barley was just lying there," said Ruthie. "It seemed wrong to let it go to waste."

"Hmm," said Bjorn, which from Bjorn was practically a standing ovation.

Every day Ruthie was up before dawn and out on the slopes. She gleaned the forgotten corners, the blown-away bundles, the frost-buried tufts that nobody else had time to find. And every evening she came back to the lodge, snow dusted and cheerful, and added her baskets to the growing collection.

The other animals started helping. Barnaby organized a snack station so Ruthie wouldn't skip lunch. ("Skipping lunch is a crime against crackers," he declared.) Finnegan tried to help glean but kept accidentally snowboarding into the barley piles, so Willa reassigned him to "enthusiastic cheering from a safe distance." Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff, the bear cubs, appointed themselves Ruthie's official basket carriers, though they spent more time riding in the baskets than carrying them.

"This is the fastest ride in the valley!" shouted Teddy, as Tilda pushed a basket-sled down a small hill.

"That's not what baskets are for!" Ruthie called after them, laughing.

On the eighth evening, Nora Snowmane found Ruthie sitting alone by the lodge fire, mending a torn basket by lantern light.

"You know," said Nora softly, "when I told you that you could go anywhere -- find a bigger valley, a warmer place -- I meant it. You didn't have to follow an old bear to a town you'd never seen."

Ruthie tied off her thread and looked up. "Nora, you were kind to me when my home washed away. You shared your last biscuit with me in the rain. You told me stories when I couldn't sleep." She paused. "Where you go, I go. I already said that. I'm not very good at un-saying things."

Nora's old eyes glistened. She pulled Ruthie into a warm bear hug that smelled like pine needles and peppermint.

The night before the festival, Barnaby did a final count of the barley stores. He counted twice. Then three times. Then he burst out of the storage barn so fast he tripped over his own flippers.

"We have *more* than enough!" he shouted. "We have enough for the festival AND a surplus! Ruthie's gleaning saved the whole harvest!"

The cheer that went up could be heard all the way to Frost Mountain.

At the Winter Festival, Herschel brewed the biggest batch of barley-butter cocoa in Frostpeak history. The mugs were enormous. The marshmallows were the size of snowballs. Bjorn Bigpaws stood on the lodge steps and made a short speech, which for Bjorn meant exactly eleven words:

"Ruthie came as a stranger. She leaves tonight as family. Welcome home."

The whole valley stomped and clapped and hooted. Finnegan set off a confetti cannon he'd apparently been hiding in his feathers. Cleo had painted a banner that read "RUTHIE OF FROSTPEAK" in letters made of tiny pressed barley flowers.

Ruthie stood in the middle of it all, her tin-can lantern glowing at her hip, her striped scarf fluttering, and for the first time in a very long while, she didn't feel like a newcomer.

She felt like she was exactly where she was supposed to be.

Later, when the aurora draped across the sky like a quilt made of green and purple light, Ruthie and Nora sat side by side on the lodge porch, sipping cocoa.

"So," said Nora. "Still glad you came?"

Ruthie leaned her head against the old bear's arm. "Where you go, I go," she said. "That's that."

And the snow fell gently, gently, like the valley itself was tucking them in.
