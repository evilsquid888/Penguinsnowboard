# Story 22: Abby and the Star-Count Camp
### *Inspired by God's Promise to Abraham — Genesis 15:1–6*
**Characters:** Willa Wobble, Nora Snowmane, Abby Arctic-Hare  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Stargazer Ridge  
**Theme:** Hope while waiting

---

## Opening Wink

*Revision Pass 3 Spark:* When Abby finally counted the stars, she ran out of numbers and had to invent some new ones.

Frostpeak had one rule for evenings like this: cocoa first, courage second, and snacks always nearby. Abby Arctic-Hare had an additional rule: dreams first, doubts never. This was harder than it sounds when your dream feels impossible and the valley is very large and you are a medium-sized hare with very large ears and a very big worry.

## The Story

**Abby Arctic-Hare** had a dream so large it frightened her.

She kept it tucked inside her coat pocket like a folded-up map to somewhere she wasn't sure existed. Some days she'd take it out and look at it. Other days she kept it firmly in her pocket and didn't look at all, because looking at an impossible dream is sometimes the hardest thing a person can do.

The dream was this: a summer camp on Stargazer Ridge for young animals who had never seen the northern lights.

Not a small camp — a *real* one. With tents and telescopes and teaching and three proper meals and a firepit and a telescope tower and a permanent building someday, maybe. A place where any cub, any foal, any hatchling from anywhere in the valley or beyond could come and spend a week learning to read the stars.

The problem was everything.

Abby had no money, no building, no telescope, no teachers confirmed, no equipment, no permission from the Ridge Council, and — most pressingly — she had never actually run a camp before. She had attended camps. She had helped at camps. But running one was a different animal entirely, and Abby was acutely aware that she was one medium-sized hare with enormous ears and a folded-up dream.

"You look," said **Willa Wobble**, finding her one evening on the bench outside the Warming Hut, "like someone sitting inside a worry."

Abby unfolded her hands in her lap. "I want to do something that seems impossible."

"What kind of impossible?" Willa asked, sitting beside her.

"Numbers-impossible. I've done the calculations. To run a proper camp I need eight things I don't have."

"How many things do you have?"

"Two and a half."

"Which two and a half?"

Abby counted on her paws: "The dream. The location — well, I don't *have* the location, but I know which one. And the half is that I know what the camp should feel like, which isn't a thing exactly, but it's something."

Willa considered this. "Tell me about what it should feel like."

So Abby told her. She talked about the first night of camp, when the new arrivals would be nervous and the sky would go dark and the fires would come up and then — *then* — the ridge would bloom with stars and every small face would tip upward and the entire mountain would be lit with wonder. She talked about the telescopes and the star charts and the way the constellations had names that told stories, and how knowing those stories made the darkness feel inhabited instead of empty.

She talked for a long time. She hadn't meant to. But once the dream was out of her pocket it kept unfolding.

When she stopped, Willa was quiet.

"How many young animals are in Frostpeak Valley right now," Willa said finally, "who have never been to Stargazer Ridge after dark?"

Abby thought. "At least forty. Probably more. Most families don't come up there because it's a long hike."

"And in neighboring valleys?"

"Hundreds, I'd imagine."

Willa looked up at the sky above them — dark blue, already scattered with early stars, the Milky Way just beginning to appear like spilled silver dust. "Look up," she said.

Abby looked up.

"Try to count them," Willa said.

Abby started counting. She got to thirty-seven and lost her place and started again and lost her place at fifty-something and started again and by the third time the stars were coming out faster than she could count and the sky was getting bigger and bigger until it was so full of stars she couldn't see where one ended and another began.

"I can't," she said. "There are too many."

"That's not a problem," said Willa. "That's the answer. The camp doesn't need to be big enough for all the stars. It needs to start big enough for the first one."

---

**Nora Snowmane** found them both still sitting there an hour later, necks tipped back, not talking.

"What are you doing?" Nora asked.

"Counting stars," said Abby. "I keep losing count."

"Good," said Nora, sitting down. "Means there are more than you expected."

Abby turned to look at her. "I want to run a star camp on Stargazer Ridge and I don't have enough resources."

"What do you have?" Nora asked.

"A dream. A location. And something like a feeling."

Nora thought about this for approximately four seconds. "I'll donate the first tent," she said. "I have a large one from the summer expedition. It fits twelve cubs, possibly more if they're friendly."

Abby blinked. "Just — like that?"

"Just like that," Nora said. "What else do you need?"

"A telescope. A firepit permit from the Ridge Council. Supplies for four nights. Someone who knows the star charts. A cook who—"

"I'll ask Barnaby about cooking," Nora said. "He'll say yes and also provide fish crackers whether you ask for them or not, so it's best to just accept that now."

"The Ridge Council permit is the hardest part. They never approve new things."

"Bjorn is on the council."

Abby stared. "He is?"

"Has been for six years. He's very quiet about it, but he does an excellent job." Nora smiled. "He'll listen to you, Abby. Tell him what you told us. Not the numbers — the feeling."

---

The first **Stargazer Ridge Star Camp** ran the following autumn, three weeks after the frost came in.

It was smaller than Abby's largest dream — twelve cubs from four valleys, two donated tents, one telescope that Herschel had found in his attic and which had a small crack in the casing that you had to hold just right, one firepit that Bjorn had approved (with conditions, three forms, and a very specific fire-safety talk that he gave himself, very seriously, while the cubs ate fish crackers and Barnaby passed seconds).

But on the third night, when the clouds that had been threatening finally moved on and the sky opened up enormous and brilliant above Stargazer Ridge — fourteen small faces tipped upward at once.

Abby stood at the back and watched them look.

A wolf cub from the Eastern Valley grabbed her neighbor's paw without thinking, just needing to share the enormity of it.

A little bear covered his mouth with both paws and said "Oh."

One of the younger cubs, who had been sleepy all evening and seemed unlikely to make it past dinner, stood straight up and said, loudly and seriously: "I did not know the sky was that large."

"Nobody does," Abby said softly, "until they see it."

Willa appeared beside her with two mugs of cocoa. "Is it what you imagined?"

Abby took the mug. She looked at the stars. She looked at the cubs. She looked at the stars again.

"It's better," she said.

Up on the ridge, far above the treeline, the northern lights made their entrance — green and violet, slow-rolling and magnificent — and twelve small voices went *oh* at exactly the same moment, all together, the sound of wonder that is the same in every language and every valley and at every age.

Abby held her mug with both paws and let the warmth seep into her fingers.

Some dreams, once you start walking toward them, grow into something larger than you planned.

Not smaller.

Larger.
