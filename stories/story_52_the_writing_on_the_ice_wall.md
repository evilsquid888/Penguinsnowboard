# Story 52: The Writing on the Ice Wall
### *Inspired by Writing on the Wall — Daniel 5*
**Characters:** Bela Bear, Bjorn Bigpaws  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Ice Palace  
**Theme:** Pride has limits

## The Story

**Bela Bear** had won the Ice Palace Gala Championship four years in a row, and she wanted everyone to know.

This was not subtle. The Ice Palace — a grand domed structure of carved ice on the north side of Frostpeak Valley, used for official skating events and very impressive parties — had, over the past month, been decorated with Bela's trophies, Bela's portraits, Bela's personal coat-of-arms (which she had designed herself and which featured two skating blades crossed over a large letter B), and seven banners depicting Bela's finest championship moments, ordered chronologically.

"Is that a new portrait?" Bjorn Bigpaws asked, arriving at the Gala and looking at a particularly large painting near the entrance.

"My third this season," said Bela, with the casual pride of someone who had spent considerable time deciding it wasn't too many.

The Gala Skating Night was the premiere social event of the Ice Palace calendar — a celebration of the season, with music and lights and spectacular figure skating and soup from Barnaby and cocoa in the fancy cups that only came out for special occasions. Everyone was invited. Everyone came. It was genuinely wonderful.

Or it had been, in previous years.

This year, the trophies and banners had taken up so much space that three of the Warming Corner tables had been removed to fit them, and the Nestling Flock had nowhere to sit during the soup portion. The music was also exclusively songs about Bela's championships. (The Kingfisher Choir had been handed a specific setlist.) By the third verse of the second trophy-themed anthem, Beryl the narwhal's orange scarf had begun twitching in the way it did when she was being very patient about something.

Then the ice wrote something.

It happened mid-event, during Bela's triumphant solo skate. She was in the center of the rink, completing a very impressive sequence of turns, when a collective gasp went around the room. Everyone was looking not at Bela, but at the far ice wall.

Letters had appeared there. Large, careful letters, carved into the ice by what looked like no tool anyone could see — as if the cold itself had decided to leave a note.

The letters were in Old Valley Runic, which almost nobody in Frostpeak could read anymore.

Almost nobody, except **Bjorn Bigpaws**.

Bjorn could read Old Valley Runic because his grandmother had taught him one winter when he was young and they'd been snowed in together for eleven days with nothing else to do. He hadn't used it in years. It was not a skill that came up often.

He walked to the ice wall and read the letters carefully, his nose very close to the surface.

"Well?" said Bela, who had stopped skating and whose magnificent expression had gone somewhat uncertain. "What does it say?"

Bjorn straightened up. He was quiet for a moment, which was not like him.

"It says," he said carefully, "that pride is a wonderful guest but a terrible landlord. That trophies are for remembering what you did, not for filling all the space so there's no room for anyone else. And—" he checked one more time to make sure he had it right "—that your guests have nowhere to sit, and the soup is getting cold."

A silence.

Bela looked at the banners. She looked at the seven portraits. She looked at the Nestling Flock, who were indeed standing in a clump near the entrance looking uncertain.

She looked at Bjorn.

"Is that *exactly* what it says?"

"The Old Valley Runic is quite specific about the soup," said Bjorn.

Another silence. Bela's expression did several things in quick succession that were difficult to catalog. Pride. Defensiveness. Embarrassment. And then, slowly, something more honest.

"The tables," she said finally. "We should put the tables back."

"Yes," said Bjorn. "And perhaps — a few of the banners could go in storage."

"Perhaps," said Bela. Her voice was careful. "Not all of them."

"Of course not all of them. Just—"

"Perhaps four."

"Four seems right," said Bjorn.

The tables went back. The banners came down from the Nestling Flock's corner of the room. The soup was reheated and served, and the Kingfisher Choir, liberated from the trophy-anthem setlist, played something actually enjoyable, and the evening became what it was supposed to be: a genuine celebration, with room in it for everyone.

Bela skated brilliantly, as she always did. When the crowd applauded her at the end, it felt different — warmer, somehow — than the applause for the championship banners had.

She was quiet on the way home, and when she passed the ice wall, the writing was gone. Just smooth, clear ice, reflecting the torchlight.

"Was it real?" she asked Bjorn.

"The tables were empty and now they're full," said Bjorn. "The Nestling Flock is finishing their soup. That seems real enough to me."

Bela pulled her scarf tighter. "Four banners," she said quietly, as if reminding herself.

"Four banners," agreed Bjorn. "Entirely enough."
