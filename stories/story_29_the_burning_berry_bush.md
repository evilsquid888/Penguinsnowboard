# Story 29: The Burning Berry Bush
### *Inspired by The Burning Bush — Exodus 3*
**Characters:** Mo Mountain-Goat, Piper Paddlefoot  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Ember Pine Grove  
**Theme:** Listening and saying yes

---

## Opening Wink

*Revision Pass 3 Spark:* The bush glowed so warmly that three passing snow finches stopped to warm their toes, completely unaware this was a spiritual moment.

If moonbeams had mittens, this would be the night they came out to clap for the valley. Mo Mountain-Goat had been tending the quiet back trails of Frostpeak Valley for years, asking for nothing, expecting less, which is exactly the kind of resume you bring when you're not expecting to be called.

## The Story

**Mo Mountain-Goat** was the most quietly useful creature in Frostpeak Valley.

He didn't race. He didn't compete. He showed up at dawn with his old wooden trail-grooming frame strapped to his back and he smoothed the beginner paths and cleared the icy patches that appeared overnight and replaced the marker flags that blew away in wind. He did this every morning, had done it for years, and expected very little in return except for Herschel's early-morning coffee that always appeared on the Warming Hut step before he started his route.

He was, in his own estimation, ordinary.

Not boring — he had a fine wit and strong opinions about snow texture — but ordinary. A maintenance goat. A background character. He had long ago made peace with this, because someone had to groom the beginner trails and someone had to replace the flags and someone had to be the kind of person who showed up before anyone was watching.

On a Tuesday in the deep cold of February, Mo was grooming the far path through Ember Pine Grove when he noticed the light.

It came from a low berry bush at the trail's edge — one of the winter holly bushes that dotted the grove, their red berries bright against the snow. The light was gold and warm, the color of embers or candlelight, flickering very slightly, as though something inside the bush was breathing.

Mo stopped.

He looked at the bush for a long moment.

The bush was not on fire. He checked. The berries were intact. The twigs and stems were cold and normal. But the light was unmistakably there — warm and particular and present.

"Hm," said Mo. He poked at it with his trail pole. The light continued, unimpressed by the pole.

He took off his hat.

*Mo*, said something. Not loudly. Very quietly. In the way that certain truths are quiet — not because they're unsure of themselves, but because they don't need to shout.

Mo's hat stayed off.

"Who's there?" he said.

*I've been watching you groom these trails for years*, the something said. Mo couldn't tell later if he'd heard it or felt it — it arrived in his chest rather than his ears. *You think you're ordinary. You're not. I need someone exactly like you for something important.*

Mo looked at the glowing bush. He looked at his trail pole. He looked at the path stretching ahead of him, ordinary and snow-covered and deeply familiar.

"Me?" he said.

*You.*

"I groom trails."

*You make sure everyone can walk the path. That's exactly the work I need.*

Mo thought about this. "What would I have to do?"

*Lead a group of lost travelers home. They've been stuck on the Ridge Road for two weeks — they took the wrong fork and can't find the junction back.*

Mo had heard about them. A small group of traveler families — snow foxes, an ermine pair, two young reindeer — who'd taken the north fork on the Ridge Road and gotten turned around in the maze of unmarked paths beyond the third marker.

"Bjorn could lead them," Mo said. "Bjorn knows those paths better than I do."

*Bjorn is leading the valley council this week. And Piper would race them and leave half of them behind. And Finnegan would get them more lost.*

"Nora—"

*I know your objections. I know you think you're the wrong choice. You're not. Will you go?*

Mo looked at the berry bush with its warm, impossible light. He looked at his trail pole. He thought about two weeks of lost travelers in the cold and the particular ache he felt when he knew a path was blocked and people couldn't get through.

He put his hat back on.

"Fine," he said. "I'll go. But I'm taking Piper as backup."

The light in the bush brightened for just a moment — warm and pleased — and then it faded gently, like a candle carried out of the room, and the holly bush was just a holly bush again, red-berried and frost-tipped and ordinary.

---

**Piper Paddlefoot** said yes immediately, because Piper said yes to most things that involved adventure, and "rescue mission to the Ridge Road maze" qualified handsomely.

"You heard a voice from a berry bush?" she said as they strapped on their gear.

"I saw a light," Mo said. "And I heard something."

"What did it sound like?"

Mo considered this. "Like being known," he said finally. "Like someone who had watched for a long time and knew exactly what they were looking at."

Piper thought about this while she adjusted her goggles. "That sounds like a big deal."

"It was a very small bush," Mo said. "But yes. It was a big deal."

The Ridge Road maze was three hours north, through the deep part of Ember Pine Grove where the trails narrowed and crossed each other at confusing angles that changed with snowfall. Mo had groomed the beginner trails for years, but he'd also memorized the maintenance paths — the routes the trail crew used to reach every corner of the system. He knew a way through the maze that most creatures didn't know existed.

They found the lost travelers at the third junction: eight animals in varying states of cold and tired and cranky, arranged around a small fire that was bravely but unsuccessfully trying to heat four square meters of mountain air.

A snow fox stood up when she saw them. "Oh, thank goodness. We've been here since—"

"I know," Mo said. "Two weeks. I'm Mo. I know the back path out. It's about two hours. Can everyone move?"

They could.

Mo led. He didn't race — he moved at the pace of the slowest traveler, a young reindeer who had a slightly swollen knee from a stumble. Piper skated ahead to check junctions and called back directions. At the confusing crossings, Mo placed his trail pole to mark the right turn.

*Step. Choose. Step.*

Two hours and eleven minutes later, the group emerged from the grove onto the main valley road, the Warming Hut's lights visible a mile below.

The snow fox sat down in the snow and put her face in her paws for a moment. When she looked up, her eyes were bright. "Thank you. How did you know where we were?"

Mo thought about the glowing bush and the quiet voice and the way it had known things about him that he'd never quite put into words.

"A good source," he said.

On the walk down, Piper fell into step beside him. "You were the right choice," she said.

Mo adjusted his trail pole. "The bush said so."

"I believe it," said Piper. "Also, next time a bush talks to you, can I be there? Just once? I'd like to see that."

Mo smiled. "I'll put in a request."

"How?"

"I'll groom the trail past it extra carefully."

Piper laughed — her big, full laugh that carried across snowy valleys. "That's the most Mo answer possible."

"Thank you," said Mo. "I take that as a compliment."

The valley lights bloomed below them, warm and gold and welcoming, and eight lost travelers walked into their warmth with a maintenance goat leading the way.
