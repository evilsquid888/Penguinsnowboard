# Story 27: The Grain-Barn Blizzard Plan
### *Inspired by Joseph Saves for Famine — Genesis 41*
**Characters:** Bjorn Bigpaws, Herschel the Walrus  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Supply Slope  
**Theme:** Wise preparation

---

## Opening Wink

Some nights the wind whistles. Tonight it howled like it was practicing for something big. Very, very big.

## The Story

Bjorn Bigpaws woke up on a Tuesday morning with a dream stuck in his head like a snowball wedged behind his ear. He sat on the edge of his enormous bed (polar-bear-sized, naturally) and stared at the wall.

"Seven fat snowmen," he murmured. "Then seven skinny ones. Then the skinny ones *ate* the fat ones."

He shook his great furry head. "That can't be right."

But the dream came again on Wednesday. And Thursday. Each time, seven plump, jolly snowmen stood in a row on Supply Slope, grinning their pebble grins. Then seven scrawny, sad-looking snowmen appeared behind them, and one by one, the skinny snowmen swallowed the fat ones whole, like fuzzy white snakes unhinging their jaws. By the end, only the seven skinny snowmen remained, looking hungrier than ever.

By Friday, Bjorn had had enough. He trudged through the powdery morning snow to Herschel's cocoa lodge, where the old walrus was already stirring a pot big enough to bathe in.

"Herschel," Bjorn said, ducking through the doorway (he always had to duck), "I keep having the strangest dream."

"Sit down and tell me about it," Herschel said, pouring cocoa into a bowl-sized mug. "And drink this. Nobody should interpret dreams on an empty stomach."

Bjorn described the snowmen in detail -- the fat, happy ones with their carrot noses and top hats, and the skinny, shivering ones with nothing but twigs for arms.

Herschel set down his ladle. His whiskers twitched. The old walrus had seen many winters, and he knew how to read the language of snow and sky the way others read books.

"Seven fat snowmen," Herschel said slowly. "That sounds like seven good winters. Plenty of snow for boarding, plenty of fish in the streams, plenty of berries on the bushes. Seven skinny snowmen? Seven lean winters. Cold without the fun. Wind without the powder. Empty pantries and bare slopes."

Bjorn's eyes went wide. "Seven *bad* winters? Coming *here*?"

"Dreams like that don't repeat for no reason, Bjorn. I'd listen if I were you."

So Bjorn called a valley meeting. Everyone gathered at Supply Slope -- Piper Paddlefoot bouncing on her board with impatience, Barnaby Beaksworth clutching a bag of fish crackers like a security blanket, Willa Wobble with her notebook ready, Finnegan Flipsworth somehow already tangled in his own scarf, and Cleo Coldwater sketching the crowd while she waited.

"Friends," Bjorn announced, standing on a supply crate so everyone could see him (which was mostly unnecessary, given that he was a polar bear), "I believe a terrible blizzard season is coming. Maybe not this year or next. But soon. And we need to prepare."

Silence. Then Finnegan spoke up.

"How soon is soon? Like, soon-soon, or soon-but-actually-later-soon?"

"Soon enough that we should start now," Bjorn said firmly.

"Start what, exactly?" asked Piper, one foot already on her snowboard.

"Storing supplies. Building a grain barn -- well, a fish-and-berry-and-cocoa barn. Stocking it during the good years so we're ready for the hard ones."

More silence. Then Barnaby raised his flipper. "Did you say... a cocoa barn?"

"Among other things, yes."

"I'm in," said Barnaby immediately.

But not everyone was convinced. Magnus Meltsworth lumbered forward, his permanent frown even frowner than usual. "We've never needed a supply barn before. The valley provides. Why waste good sledding time building storage sheds?"

"Because the valley won't always provide this much," Bjorn said gently. "That's what the dream was telling me."

"Dreams," Magnus scoffed. "I dreamed last week that I was a butterfly. Didn't mean I started growing wings."

A few animals laughed nervously. But Herschel stepped forward, and when Herschel spoke, people listened, because Herschel had been in the valley longer than anyone and his cocoa recipe was a sacred trust.

"I've seen lean winters," the walrus said quietly. "When I was young, a blizzard came that lasted three weeks straight. No fishing. No foraging. The slopes were walls of ice. We survived, but barely. If Bjorn says we should prepare, I say we prepare."

That settled it -- mostly. Over the next several seasons, the valley went to work. Willa Wobble drew up plans for the grain barn, which they built into the hillside at Supply Slope, insulated with packed snow and pine branches. It had seven chambers, one for each good year's worth of supplies.

During the first good year, they stored dried fish and smoked salmon. Barnaby was in charge of quality control, which meant he tasted everything. Twice. "Just to be sure," he explained, his beak covered in crumbs.

During the second good year, they stocked berries -- frost berries, sugar berries, the tiny purple ones that grew near Ember Pine Grove. Cleo painted labels for every jar, turning the barn shelves into an art gallery.

The third year brought cocoa beans, which Herschel roasted and stored in cedar boxes. The fourth year, they added nuts and seeds. The fifth, dried mushrooms and root vegetables. The sixth, firewood and lamp oil. And during the seventh good year, they topped off everything and added a final treasure: three enormous barrels of Herschel's Secret Recipe Hot Cocoa Mix, sealed with wax and labeled *BREAK OPEN IN CASE OF TERRIBLE BLIZZARD*.

"That's a lot of cocoa," observed Piper.

"There is no such thing as too much cocoa," Herschel replied, and that was the end of that discussion.

Meanwhile, life in Frostpeak Valley was wonderful. The slopes were perfect, the weather was crisp, and every evening the friends gathered at the lodge to swap stories. Some animals forgot about the supply barn entirely. Even Magnus started to look a little smug. "Seven years of storing food for a blizzard that never came," he grumbled to anyone who'd listen. "What a waste."

Then, on a quiet morning in the eighth year, Willa Wobble looked at her barometer and went very still.

"Bjorn," she said. "You need to see this."

The barometer's needle had dropped lower than Willa had ever seen it. The sky to the north was a color that didn't have a proper name -- not gray, not purple, but something in between that made your bones feel cold just looking at it.

The blizzard arrived that afternoon.

It was not a polite blizzard. It did not knock first. It roared over the mountains like a living thing, burying the slopes in minutes, shaking the lodge walls, turning the world into a howling white blur. Wind screamed through the valley so loud that Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff hid under their mother Nora's belly with their paws over their ears.

"Everyone to the grain barn!" Bjorn bellowed through the storm.

They huddled inside the hillside shelter, all of them -- penguins, bears, Herschel, a family of seals, even Magnus, who said nothing as he squeezed through the doorway. The barn was snug and dry and packed to the rafters with food.

The blizzard lasted ten days.

Ten days of wind and ice and darkness. But inside the grain barn, it was almost cozy. Barnaby rationed the fish and berries (though Finnegan kept sneaking extra crackers when he thought nobody was looking -- everybody was looking). Cleo taught the little ones to paint with berry juice on scraps of birch bark. Piper organized snowboard-trick lectures using pinecones as demonstration models. Willa read stories aloud by lantern light. And every evening, Herschel cracked open one of his sacred cocoa barrels and made a pot so rich and warm that even the howling wind outside seemed to quiet down for a sip.

On the fifth night, Magnus Meltsworth sat down heavily beside Bjorn. For a long time, the old bear said nothing. Then he cleared his throat.

"That dream of yours," Magnus said.

"Yes?"

"Good dream."

That was the closest Magnus Meltsworth had ever come to saying thank you, and Bjorn understood it perfectly. He poured Magnus an extra-large cocoa and they sat together, listening to the storm, feeling grateful for walls and warmth and seven years of patience.

When the blizzard finally broke on the eleventh morning, sunlight poured into the valley like liquid gold. The animals stumbled outside, blinking, and found the world transformed -- fresh powder three feet deep, every tree branch frosted in crystal, the sky scrubbed so clean it hurt to look at.

"The slopes," Piper whispered, her eyes huge. "The slopes are going to be *incredible*."

And they were. That day, the valley had the best powder run in living memory, and every single animal -- from the smallest penguin chick to Bjorn himself -- carved at least one perfect turn through snow that sparkled like diamonds.

That evening, gathered around the lodge fire, Herschel raised his mug. "To Bjorn," he said. "Who listened to a strange dream and had the courage to act on it."

"To the grain barn!" Barnaby added.

"To the cocoa barrels!" shouted Finnegan.

"To each other," Bjorn said simply.

They clinked mugs -- ceramic against ceramic, a sound like tiny bells -- and outside the window, the first stars of evening appeared above Supply Slope, twinkling like they'd been saving up their sparkle for just this moment.
