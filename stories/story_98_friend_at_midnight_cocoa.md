# Story 98: Friend at Midnight Cocoa
### *Inspired by Friend at Midnight — Luke 11*
**Characters:** Coco Crow, Barnaby Beaksworth  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Midnight Alley  
**Theme:** Persistent kindness

---

## Opening Wink

Frostpeak had one rule for evenings like this: cocoa first, courage second, and snacks always nearby.

## The Story

**Coco Crow** knocked on Barnaby's door at midnight on a Thursday, which was not a reasonable hour to knock on anyone's door, and she knew it.

She had unexpected guests.

Not unusual guests — unexpected ones. Her cousin from the eastern settlement and his family of four had arrived at her door two hours ago, travel-weary and hungry, having underestimated how long the cross-mountain journey would take in the late-season conditions. They had not been able to send word ahead. They were hungry. Coco had given them everything she had in her cottage — all of it, generously and gladly — and it had not been quite enough, because she had been planning to restock in the morning.

She stood at Barnaby's door at midnight in the cold, feeling deeply awkward about the hour and deeply certain she had no other option.

She knocked.

A light appeared in the window after a moment. Then the sound of careful footsteps. Then the door opened.

**Barnaby Beaksworth** stood in the doorway in his nightcap and an expression that was, first, briefly surprised, and then immediately and genuinely attentive.

"Coco," he said.

"I'm sorry," said Coco. "I know it's midnight. My cousin arrived unexpectedly — family, four of them — I gave them everything I had and it wasn't enough and I—" she stopped. "I'm sorry. I know this is—"

"What do they need?" said Barnaby.

Not *why didn't they plan better* or *this is a strange hour* or *can this wait until morning*. Just: what do they need.

"Something warm," said Coco. "Enough for four adults plus my cousin's young ones. Whatever you have."

Barnaby was already turning back into the cottage.

"Come in," he said. "Give me ten minutes."

---

He did not have soup made — he rarely left soup out at midnight unless he was expecting late guests. But he had everything needed to make soup, because Barnaby always had everything needed to make soup, and he had it organized in a way that made ten minutes genuinely achievable.

Coco stood in his kitchen and watched him work: the stock, already warm from the banked fire, the vegetables cut quickly and correctly, the seasoning added with the practiced certainty of someone who had made this soup a hundred times and could do it half-asleep at midnight without compromising the result.

"You don't have to—" she started.

"I know," said Barnaby. "I want to."

"I feel terrible waking you up."

Barnaby looked at her from the stove. "Coco. Your cousin traveled all day and arrived hungry and you came to get food for them. That is what neighbors are for."

"But at midnight—"

"Need doesn't keep business hours," said Barnaby. "Neither do I."

He said it simply, without drama, as if it were obvious. She thought about how it would have felt to have knocked on any other door in the valley at midnight — the apology that would have been required, the guilt, the sense of imposing. Barnaby's kitchen felt, at midnight with an unexpected soup in progress, like the most ordinary and welcome place she'd been all week.

---

She carried the soup back to her cottage in three covered pots, warmed by the walk.

Her cousin's family were sitting in the warm light of her cottage, tired and grateful, and when she set the pots on the table their expressions shifted to something that she recognized as the specific relief of unexpected welcome.

The youngest one — a very small crow who had been almost asleep in a chair — woke up at the smell of the soup and said "*what is that*" with enormous reverence.

"Barnaby's soup," said Coco. "From our neighbor."

"At midnight?"

"At midnight," said Coco. "He didn't mind."

She meant this entirely. Barnaby had not minded. He had answered the door and asked what they needed and made the soup and handed it over and said *come back if you need more* in the tone of someone who genuinely meant it.

Persistent kindness doesn't wait for a convenient hour. It answers the door and asks what's needed and starts the soup. It does not keep business hours.
