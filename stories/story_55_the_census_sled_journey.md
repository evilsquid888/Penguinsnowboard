# Story 55: The Census Sled Journey
### *Inspired by Journey to Bethlehem — Luke 2:1–5*
**Characters:** Miri Marmot, Jo Moose, Mary Moose  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Bethlehem Bend  
**Theme:** Faithful travel

---

## Opening Wink

Some nights the wind whistles; tonight it hummed like it knew a secret worth sharing.

## The Story

The Valley Census happened every five years, and it required every resident of Frostpeak to travel to their family's registered settlement to be counted.

For most creatures, this was a short trip — a day's ski, an overnight at a warming hut, back by the weekend.

For **Jo Moose** and **Mary Moose**, it was more complicated.

Mary was expecting their first calf, due in approximately three weeks — which the valley's most experienced midwife, a round badger named Wren, had called "close enough to be watching carefully." Jo had looked at the census schedule, then at Mary, then at the census schedule again with the expression of a creature doing very difficult math.

"Bethlehem Bend is a two-day sled journey," he said.

"Yes," said Mary.

"You're expecting in three weeks."

"I know that."

"Wren said close enough to be watching carefully."

"Jo," said Mary, gently, "I'm a moose. We manage."

The journey would not be comfortable. But they had a good sled, and the route to Bethlehem Bend was well-maintained, and there was one established warming hut at the midpoint. And **Miri Marmot** — who had been running the valley's sled-route guide service for twelve years and had an unbroken record of getting every client where they were going on time — had agreed to guide them.

Miri was small, round, and extremely competent. She had three backup route maps, two emergency blankets, the good wax for the sled runners, and a flask of Barnaby's soup broth in a sealed container. She had guided pregnant creatures before. She had a plan for everything.

"Ready?" she said, on the morning of departure.

"Ready," said Jo.

"Ready," said Mary, settling into the sled's passenger seat with the careful dignity of someone carrying something very important.

"Good," said Miri. "I'll set a pace that's smooth, not fast. We'll reach the midpoint hut by afternoon."

---

The first day was beautiful and hard.

Beautiful because Frostpeak in midwinter was always beautiful — the snow deep and white, the trees draped in crystal, the light coming low and gold across the ridges. Hard because the census route was long and some of the passes were narrow, and the sled was heavy, and Jo pulled steadily through all of it without complaint, his great moose strength turning the difficult parts into merely effortful ones.

Mary watched the landscape and told Jo what she saw, which was her way of traveling — narrating the beauty as they moved through it, so the journey became a kind of story.

"The light is hitting the east ridge," she said. "It looks like the whole mountain is made of something warm."

"It looks like hard work from here," said Jo. But he was smiling.

Miri checked her maps and her pace and her route markers, and at one point detoured around a patch of icy crust that looked fine but felt wrong underfoot — ten years of instinct telling her to take the longer way — and it was right, because when they returned to the main path the ice patch had a crack in it that would have been bad news for the sled runners.

They reached the midpoint hut by mid-afternoon.

The hut keeper, an ancient lynx named Sylvester, took one look at Mary and produced extra blankets from somewhere in the back and put the largest kettle on the fire.

"How far along?" he said.

"Three weeks, approximately," said Mary.

Sylvester made a sound that was not quite worry and not quite calm. "We'll see you're well rested."

He fed them soup and showed them the warmest corner of the hut and turned the fire up higher than it usually went, and said nothing else about it, which was the correct amount of fuss.

---

The second day was harder.

A front had moved in overnight — not a storm, just a cold that made everything stiffer and the snow harder and the sky a deep flat grey. Miri was up at dawn checking the route and adjusting her plan: slower pace, more rest stops, a different line through the upper pass that was longer but kinder on the sled.

Jo pulled. Mary rested and watched and narrated the grey beauty of the cloud-sky. Miri checked her maps every half hour.

They came down into Bethlehem Bend in the early afternoon, the grey sky breaking just enough to let in a long shaft of gold light that lay across the settlement like a welcome.

The census office was small and warm and run by a cheerful hedgehog named Toll who stamped their names in the ledger with great satisfaction.

"You made it," said Toll. "Just the two of you?"

"Three, soon," said Mary, and Toll's expression did something very warm.

"Well then," said Toll. "We'd better make sure you're well looked after until you can safely travel back."

The settlement of Bethlehem Bend was not large, but it was the kind of place that took care of those who arrived in need. A family gave them a warm room — the best room, the one with the low stone hearth and the good, thick walls. Sylvester's brother, who lived in Bethlehem Bend, arrived the next morning with extra firewood, unbidden.

Miri Marmot stayed for two days to make sure the return journey would be possible. She sat in the evenings with the fire and her maps and her flask of broth, quiet and thorough, planning the way home.

"Will it be safe?" said Jo, on the second evening, meaning the return.

"I've gotten every client home," said Miri. "All of them. I don't plan to change that now."

"It might be three of us instead of two."

Miri looked at the fire. "I brought extra blankets," she said. "Just in case."

She had, of course. She'd packed them at the start.

Some journeys ask more than you expect. But when you go step by step, with good companions, in the direction of something important — you find you can take the steps.
