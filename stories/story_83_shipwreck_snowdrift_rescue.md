# Story 83: Shipwreck Snowdrift Rescue
### *Inspired by Paul's Shipwreck — Acts 27*
**Characters:** Paula Puffin, Bjorn Bigpaws  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Drift Coast  
**Theme:** Steady hope

## Opening Wink

Some nights the wind whistles; tonight it hummed like it knew a secret worth sharing.

## The Story

The storm hit the Drift Coast sled convoy on the second day out, and it was much worse than predicted.

Drift Coast was the northern supply route — a long flat coastal stretch used in winter for the heavy sled convoys that brought supplies from the outer settlements. **Paula Puffin** had led three successful convoys on this route. **Bjorn Bigpaws** had been on two of them. They knew the route. They had good sleds, good harnesses, a full crew of eight, and three days of emergency supplies.

None of that fully prepared them for the storm.

It came from the northwest without the usual warning signs — the pressure had felt wrong to Paula for an hour before it hit, and she'd called a halt and started securing the sleds, which was the decision that saved them. The wind came in like a wall. Visibility dropped to arm's length. The temperature fell fifteen degrees in twenty minutes.

For six hours, they held position in the storm.

The sleds were anchored. The crew sheltered in their tent configuration — four tents linked together, which Paula had practiced for exactly this scenario. They were cold. They were worried. Two younger members of the crew were genuinely frightened, in the way that storms make you frightened when you can't see and don't know when it will stop.

Paula moved through the linked tents, calmly, repeatedly.

Not pretending the storm wasn't happening. Just — present. Available. She said *we're holding, we're secure* and she meant it and the crew could tell she meant it, which is different from saying it. She sat for twenty minutes with the two frightened crew members, one on each side, not fixing their fear but being there in it with them until the fear became slightly smaller.

Bjorn kept the anchors and checked the sled ties every ninety minutes, which meant going into the full wind four times over six hours. He did it methodically, without drama, and came back in each time and said *holding*, and the word held the crew together as much as the anchors held the sleds.

---

On the morning of the third day, the convoy reached the first outer settlement — delayed by a day, missing some supplies they'd had to lighten from the sleds during the worst hours, but whole, every member, everyone accounted for.

The outer settlement had been watching for them. Fires were lit. Food was ready. There was the particular warmth of a community that knew what a winter storm meant and was genuinely relieved to see a convoy come through.

Paula sat by one of the fires with her tea and counted her crew for the last time: all eight, eating, warming hands, beginning to tell the story already in the way you tell stories when they're over and you're safe on the other side.

"How do you stay steady?" one of the young crew members asked her. "In the middle of it. When we couldn't see anything."

Paula thought about it honestly.

"I know storms end," she said. "Not always when you want them to. Not always the way you planned. But they end. So you hold what you can hold and you take care of what's in reach and you wait."

"What if they don't end?"

"In my experience," said Paula, "the ones that feel like they won't end always do. And the getting-through is something you carry with you after, like a tool you've earned." She drank her tea. "Also Bjorn checks the anchors every ninety minutes. That helps."

Bjorn, across the fire, raised his mug without looking up.

The fire was good. The tea was hot. Outside, the sky was completely clear, the stars very sharp, the kind of night that comes after a storm with the particular freshness of air that has been through something and come out the other side.

Steady hope is the kind that doesn't require the storm to be small. It just keeps checking the anchors.
