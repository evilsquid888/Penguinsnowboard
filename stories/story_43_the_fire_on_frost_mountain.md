# Story 43: The Fire on Frost Mountain
### *Inspired by Elijah on Mount Carmel — 1 Kings 18*
**Characters:** Kara Caribou, Cleo Coldwater  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Frost Mountain Bowl  
**Theme:** Truth over showmanship

## The Story

The Signal Fire Challenge had been a Frostpeak Valley tradition for as long as anyone could remember.

Every midwinter, when the nights grew longest and the stars burned coldest, the valley held a competition: who could light the great iron beacon at the summit of Frost Mountain Bowl? The beacon sat on a stone platform at the highest point of the bowl, visible for miles in every direction, and lighting it — successfully, brightly, in front of the whole gathered valley — was considered the highest honor a creature could earn at midwinter. The light meant *winter's halfway done, spring is coming, keep going.*

It was tradition. It was important. It was also, this particular year, a complete mess.

The trouble was **Roderick the Ram**.

Roderick had arrived in Frostpeak Valley three seasons ago from somewhere far south, and he was — there was no other word for it — *theatrical*. He wore a red-and-gold scarf that he'd sewn himself and he was not shy about telling you. He had opinions about Everything, delivered at full volume, with gestures. When Roderick entered a room, the room knew it. When Roderick entered the Warming Hut, the mugs rattled.

He had announced in October that he would be lighting the beacon this year.

"I have a *method*," he told anyone who would listen, and several who had stopped trying. "A proprietary system. Developed over two winters of research. When I light that beacon, Frostpeak Valley will witness something unprecedented."

"The beacon gets lit every year," said Cleo Coldwater, who was sitting nearby with her sketchbook.

"Not *like this* it doesn't," said Roderick.

**Kara Caribou** had not announced anything.

She was a quiet, steady caribou with brown-and-cream coloring and the particular patience of someone who had learned that most things work out better when you don't make speeches about them in advance. She worked as a trail keeper on the upper mountain — the one who checked the safety lines and restaked the boundary markers when storms shifted them. She knew Frost Mountain Bowl the way you know the rooms of your own house: without thinking, by feel.

She had lit the beacon twice before, in years when the chosen lighter had fallen ill or gotten turned around in weather. Both times she had done it quietly and gone home.

**Cleo Coldwater** was not competing. Cleo was there to watch, because Cleo had learned that watching things carefully was how you found the stories worth keeping, and she had a feeling this year would be one of those.

---

The morning of the challenge was bright and bitter-cold, the kind of cold that makes your nose run immediately and your breath come out in thick puffs like a small coal engine. A crowd of maybe two hundred creatures gathered at Frost Mountain Bowl — seals and penguins and snow foxes and the two elderly narwhals who never missed a valley event, wrapped in matching orange scarves.

Roderick went first.

He had brought *things*. An assistant (a nervous young marmot named Pip who was clearly regretting his life choices). A wooden cart of supplies. Several jars of what he described as "accelerant compound, proprietary blend." A set of copper bellows. A brass horn to announce his readiness.

The horn went off. It was very loud. Three seals in the front row covered their ears. A small penguin fell off the ice ledge she'd been standing on.

"FROSTPEAK VALLEY," Roderick announced, scarf fluttering magnificently, "YOUR MIDWINTER FIRE IS ABOUT TO BE *ELEVATED*."

He began his preparations. They were elaborate. He poured things from jars. He built a structure around the beacon's base out of what appeared to be very carefully arranged pinecones. He repositioned the pinecones. He repositioned them again. He consulted a handwritten diagram he'd pulled from his satchel, frowned at it, and repositioned the pinecones a third time. Pip handed him tools and received very specific instructions about the tools in return.

Twenty minutes passed.

"He's still doing the pinecones," said Bjorn quietly, from somewhere in the crowd.

"He's on his third diagram," said Cleo, sketching rapidly.

"The pinecones aren't load-bearing," said Kara, looking at the beacon platform. "He's positioned them on the downwind side. The draft will push the flame backward."

"Should we tell him?"

Kara watched Roderick, who was now adding what looked like a decorative tin star to the top of his pinecone arrangement. "I think he's committed to the pinecones."

Roderick lit his fire.

It was, for about four seconds, very impressive — a bright whoosh of blue-and-gold flame from the accelerant compound, rising dramatically into the cold air, exactly as theatrical as promised.

Then the draft hit it.

The flame bent exactly the wrong way, exactly as Kara had predicted, and instead of catching the beacon's iron bowl it scorched a modest section of Roderick's carefully arranged pinecones and went out with a polite *pfft*.

Silence.

Then — from somewhere in the front row of seals — a single enthusiastic flipper-clap.

Then another.

Then five seals clapping together, politely and with great apparent sincerity, exactly as if they had attended a very educational performance and wanted to be encouraging about it.

Roderick stared at the unlit beacon. He stared at his remaining pinecones. He stared at the seals.

"The *draft*," he said, with dignity, "was not in the diagram."

"No," Pip agreed, very quietly.

---

Kara Caribou stepped up without fanfare.

She had one thing with her: a small birchwood firestarter she'd made herself, about the length of her forearm, with a steel striker and a pouch of tinder she'd prepared from dried moss and last autumn's birch shavings. She'd been carrying it in her jacket pocket.

She looked at the beacon for a moment. Then she looked at the mountain — the particular way the wind was running down the southern face, the way the cold was pooling in the bowl, the way the light was sitting on the ice. She had read Frost Mountain Bowl in all seasons for years. She knew which way the draft ran in midwinter. She knew where to stand.

She moved to the north side of the platform. She cleared the pinecones — gently, respectfully — from around the beacon base. She knelt in the snow.

She struck the firestarter once. Twice. On the third strike, the tinder caught.

She cupped it in both hooves and blew on it — one long, steady breath, measured and calm — and then she set it against the beacon's iron bowl where she'd packed a small nest of dry moss she'd pulled from her jacket.

The flame caught.

It grew.

It spread across the beacon's iron bowl in a clean orange wave and then *rose* — tall and strong and completely steady, climbing twenty feet into the cold midwinter sky, exactly as the signal fire was supposed to look: visible for miles, burning honest and bright.

The valley went quiet in the particular way a crowd goes quiet when something true happens in front of them.

Then it erupted.

The seals in the front row surpassed themselves. The narwhals stamped their orange-scarfed approval. Barnaby appeared from somewhere with a tray of cocoa and handed mugs to everyone within arm's reach, as was his way.

Roderick the Ram stood beside his cart of proprietary jars and looked at the burning beacon for a long moment. Then he looked at Kara, who was standing quietly to the side of the platform and brushing snow off her jacket sleeves.

"You knew about the draft," he said.

"Yes," said Kara.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

Kara was quiet for a moment. "You seemed very sure of your method. I thought perhaps I was wrong." She paused. "I wasn't wrong."

Roderick nodded slowly. He was quiet in a way that was quite different from his usual mode of being. "What's in the tinder?"

"Dried moss and birch shavings. Gathered last October when the drying conditions were good."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Roderick looked at his five proprietary jars and his copper bellows and his brass horn and the moderately scorched pinecones. He looked at the burning beacon — tall and clean and honest against the dark sky.

"Truth," said Cleo Coldwater, appearing beside them with her sketchbook, "has better aerodynamics than showmanship."

Roderick looked at her.

"I'm writing it down," said Cleo. "For posterity."

Under the blazing beacon, with the aurora beginning to appear in the deep sky above, they walked down together — Kara and Cleo and Roderick and Pip and the two orange-scarfed narwhals and the entire front row of seals. Someone passed the cocoa. Someone else started singing something low and warm. The beacon burned all night.

It always did, when it was lit with honest hands.
