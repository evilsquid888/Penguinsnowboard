# Story 37: Deborah's Drumline Downhill
### *Inspired by Deborah Leads Israel — Judges 4*
**Characters:** Deborah Dove, Nora Snowmane  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Victory Ridge  
**Theme:** Wise leadership

---

## Opening Wink

*Revision Pass 3 Spark:* When Deborah said "follow me," twelve drumlines started playing simultaneously and nobody had planned that but it was perfect anyway.

The drumline downhill had never had a leader like Deborah Dove. It had never had a leader like Deborah Dove because Deborah Dove had never led anything before, and the mountain was about to find out what happened when the right creature stepped forward at exactly the right moment.

## The Story

Victory Ridge's Annual Drumline Downhill had a tradition.

Every year, a line of skiers and boarders descended the ridge in formation to music — twelve drummers stationed along the slope, each playing a beat that built on the last, so the descent was also a piece of music, the riders moving in time to a rhythm that grew richer as they went. It was spectacular and complicated and required both athletic skill and musical timing, which meant it required one person who could hold both things in mind at once.

That person was supposed to be **Captain Bram Bearington**, who had led the Drumline Downhill for seven years.

Captain Bram had a bad knee and had told **Nora Snowmane** on Monday that he could not lead this year.

Nora had listened, nodded, thanked him, and immediately gone in search of **Deborah Dove**.

**Deborah Dove** was a grey dove who had lived in Frostpeak Valley for two seasons, quiet and precise, who played three instruments and coached the young riders on rhythm-skating and had opinions about tempo that Cleo described as "very strong and usually right." She had never led anything large. She had never wanted to lead anything large. When Nora knocked on her door, Deborah was practicing scales on a small wooden flute and the expression on her face made very clear that she was enjoying the privacy.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked yet," said Nora.

"You have the face of someone who is about to ask me to lead the Drumline Downhill."

"I do have that face," Nora admitted.

"No," said Deborah again. "Captain Bram has led it seven times. He knows the mountain, he knows the drummers, he knows the formations—"

"Bram can't. His knee."

A silence.

"What about—" Deborah started.

"The others are less qualified and more confident," Nora said. "Which is the dangerous combination. You're the most qualified, and you know it."

Deborah looked at her flute. She looked at the window. Outside, the first preparations for the Drumline Downhill were already visible — the stage crew setting up drummer stations along the ridge, the flags going up, the early skiers doing warm-up runs on the lower sections.

"What if I get it wrong?" she said. "What if my tempo is off and the whole formation falls apart in front of — everyone will be watching—"

"Deborah," said Nora. "What's the biggest thing that could go wrong?"

Deborah thought. "The second drummer station's beat is always slightly early. If the leader doesn't account for it, the whole middle section drifts out of time."

"Do you know how to account for it?"

A pause. "Yes. Anticipate it by half a beat and direct from the middle of the line rather than the front."

"Does Bram know that?"

"No. He's been compensating for it incorrectly for years. That's why the middle section always sounds slightly muddy."

Nora smiled. "I'll let you think about it."

She left. She did not push. She did not argue. She left Deborah with the window view of the ridge and the sound of preparations and the weight of knowing the thing nobody else knew.

---

Deborah showed up at the ridge at dawn.

She didn't announce it. She just appeared with her flute in her satchel and her skis on her back, and she walked to where the twelve drummers were doing their warm-up practice and she listened to them for twenty minutes without speaking.

Then she walked to the second drummer station — a young snowshoe hare named Tam who was nervous and excellent — and said quietly: "Your instinct to come in early is right. But shift it to a half-beat anticipation instead of a full beat, and telegraph it to the stations on either side. They'll follow your lead."

Tam blinked at her. "Are you — are you leading today?"

"Maybe," said Deborah.

"Captain Bram—"

"Is resting his knee. Will you trust the half-beat?"

Tam thought about it. Played it mentally. Nodded.

Deborah walked the whole ridge. She stopped at each drummer station and said one specific, useful thing — not many things, not a speech, just the one thing each drummer needed to hear. The eighth station's mallets were too heavy; she swapped them. The eleventh station's position was slightly uphill from where it should be; she moved it six feet. The fourth station's drummer had been told the wrong tempo; she corrected it and watched him play through twice.

When the riders began assembling at the top, Nora was there.

"You came," Nora said.

"Someone had to," said Deborah.

She took position at the center of the line — not the front, the center, where she could hear and see in both directions. She raised her flute. The drummers looked at her. The riders looked at her.

She counted in, four steady beats.

And then the Drumline Downhill began.

It was different from the previous seven years. Everyone felt it within the first thirty seconds. The second station's half-beat anticipation unlocked the whole middle section — the rhythm swelled instead of muddying, the formation moved in genuine unison, skis and boards and drumbeats all arriving together. The descent felt like a river finding its banks.

Riders who had done the event for years glanced at each other on the slope, eyes bright above their goggles: *what is happening and why does this feel so right?*

The crowd at the bottom went quiet as the formation descended, then louder, then very loud, until by the time the last riders came through and the final drummer struck the closing beat, the applause was the loudest Victory Ridge had heard in years.

Deborah came to a stop at the bottom, slightly breathless, skis parallel.

Nora skied up beside her. "Seven years of being slightly muddy, solved."

"It was just the second station," Deborah said.

"It was you knowing about the second station," Nora said. "And choosing to use what you knew."

Deborah was quiet for a moment. "It was terrifying."

"And?"

She looked back up the ridge, where the drums were still echoing faintly. "And excellent," she said quietly. "It was also excellent."
