# Story 15: Willa and the Midnight Lamps
### *Inspired by The Wise and Foolish Virgins — Matthew 25:1–13*
**Characters:** Willa Wobble, Cleo Coldwater, Teddy & Tilda Powderpuff  
**Setting:** Starwatch Cliff Festival Trail  
**Theme:** Readiness, quiet wisdom
**Tone:** Fun, whimsical, cozy bedtime adventure

---

## Opening Wink

*Revision Pass 3 Spark:* A polite owl blinked from the rafters with the exact expression of, “Yes, packing early was wise.”

Tonight’s lesson: the difference between “ready” and “not ready” is about four candle stubs, one windproof cloth, and a penguin who actually reads the weather forecast. Willa calls this wisdom. Finnegan calls it unfair preparedness. Teddy calls it “less room for marshmallows” and has filed a formal complaint.

## The Story

Once a year, when the winter sky turned its deepest shade of velvet blue and the stars hung so close you could almost hear them humming, Frostpeak Valley held the **Midnight Lamps Festival**.

The idea was beautifully simple. Every creature in the valley received a snow-lantern — a little glass jar with a candle inside, hung on a carved ice-handle. At the sound of the midnight bell, everyone would begin the long climb up Starwatch Ridge to the cliffs at the top. If your lantern was still lit when you arrived, you got to witness the famous Starwatch Display: the moment each winter when the northern lights and the shooting stars arrived at exactly the same time, painting the sky in ribbons of green and violet and silver.

If your lantern went out? Well, the trail was dark and icy, and you had to turn back.

Nobody wanted to turn back.

“This,” said **Willa Wobble**, spreading supplies across her bunk in the Cocoa Warming Hut, “is why we prepare.”

Willa was the smallest penguin in all of Frostpeak Valley — a Little Blue Penguin who barely came up to Piper’s elbow — but she had the kind of steady, thoughtful mind that made bigger creatures stop and listen. She was currently surrounded by: four extra candle stubs, two spare wicks, a flint striker, one square of windproof cloth, a tin of waterproof matches, and a tiny jar of rendered fish oil for emergency wick-soaking.

**Cleo Coldwater** peered through the doorway and blinked at the spread. “Willa, are you packing for a festival or a polar expedition?”

“The smallest step is still a step,” Willa said, tucking each item into her satchel with the care of a surgeon. “And the smallest preparation is still preparation.”

“I packed my sketchbook,” said Cleo, holding it up. “And three extra charcoal pencils. Because what if I want to draw the stars in *four* different styles?”

“That’s artistic readiness,” Willa said graciously.

From across the hut came a tremendous clatter as **Teddy and Tilda Powderpuff** — the twin polar bear cubs who had never once entered a room at normal volume — dumped the contents of their packs onto the floor.

Marshmallows. Approximately eleven bags of marshmallows. One squished sandwich. More marshmallows. A mitten with a hole in it. And one very confused beetle that had been hibernating inside a marshmallow bag and now looked deeply offended.

“We’re ready!” announced Teddy.

“Extremely ready!” confirmed Tilda.

Willa looked at the marshmallow mountain. “Did you pack candle supplies?”

The twins exchanged a glance.

“Candles are boring,” said Teddy.

“Marshmallows are *fuel*,” said Tilda, as though this were obvious science.

“But your lantern only runs on candle wax,” Willa said gently. “Not on marshmallow optimism.”

“We didn’t mean to forget—“ Teddy began.

“—but we were very focused on snacks,” Tilda finished.

Willa sighed the sigh of someone who had already packed extras for exactly this reason.

---

The midnight bell rang like a silver coin dropped into still water, and the whole valley stirred.

From every den and hut and hollow, creatures emerged carrying their snow-lanterns. The flames bobbed and swayed in the dark like a river of tiny stars flowing uphill. Piper Paddlefoot zipped past on her snowboard, lantern swinging wildly. “Slide into it!” she hollered, narrowly missing a signpost.

Finnegan Flipsworth hopped along behind her, his Rockhopper crest bouncing. “WOOOHOOOOO — wait, is this the right trail? There are like four trails. Which one has fewer trees to hit?”

“The one you’re NOT on,” Piper called back.

Even **Nibbles and Splash**, the seal siblings, had wriggled up from the frozen lake shore, each balancing a lantern on their nose like a circus act.

“Nibbles, your flame is sideways,” said Splash.

“Art doesn’t have a right-side-up,” said Nibbles, who had clearly been spending too much time with Cleo.

The procession was magical. Dozens of golden lights wound up the mountainside, reflecting off the snow so the whole ridge seemed to glow from within. Cleo walked near the middle, sketching by lantern-light as she climbed, which meant she bumped into roughly four creatures per minute and apologized beautifully each time. “Sorry — every slope tells a story, and this one is *gorgeous*.”

Willa walked steadily at the back, her lantern burning clean and bright, her satchel heavy with supplies.

They had been climbing for nearly an hour when the wind arrived.

It didn’t creep in. It *lunged*. A massive gust roared down from the peak like a living thing, carrying stinging ice crystals and the kind of cold that bites through fur and feathers alike. Scarves snapped sideways. The snow underfoot scattered in white sheets.

And one by one, the lanterns went out.

*Fwip.* Piper’s flame guttered and died.

*Fwip. Fwip. Fwip.* Three more along the line.

“Uh-oh,” said Teddy.

“Big uh-oh,” said Tilda.

Finnegan’s lantern didn’t just go out — it flew off the handle entirely and sailed into a snowbank, where it hissed and vanished. “That was rude,” Finnegan said to the wind, personally.

Within minutes, the glowing procession had become a dark, confused huddle of creatures standing in howling wind, holding dead lanterns and looking very sorry they hadn’t packed extras.

“My lantern!” wailed Splash. “Nibbles, relight mine!”

“Mine’s out too!” said Nibbles. “I was balancing, not *fire-proofing*!”

A low murmur of worry rippled through the group. Without lit lanterns, the trail rules said you turned back. And they were so close — Starwatch Cliff was only twenty minutes away.

Then, from the back of the line, a small, steady glow appeared.

Willa.

Her lantern burned perfectly. She had wrapped her windproof cloth around the glass at the first sign of gusting, creating a tiny shelter that let the flame breathe without letting the wind reach it. It glowed warm and golden in the blue darkness, and every creature on the trail turned toward it like flowers toward the sun.

“Right,” said Willa, kneeling in the snow and opening her satchel. “Everyone gather round. Take one, pass one.”

She worked quickly and calmly. She replaced Piper’s candle with a fresh stub and relit it from her own flame, shielding the transfer with cupped flippers. She handed Cleo a spare wick and showed her how to trim it short so it wouldn’t catch the wind. She gave Teddy and Tilda each a candle stub and a very gentle look that said *this is why we don’t pack eleven bags of marshmallows*.

“I feel judged,” whispered Teddy.

“You feel *helped*,” Willa corrected. “There’s a difference.”

Cleo held up a sketchbook page as a wind-shield while Willa relit the next lantern. Finnegan cupped his flippers around another and managed not to sneeze on it, which he considered a personal triumph.

One by one, the lights came back.

Splash and Nibbles got their nose-balanced lanterns relit and immediately resumed their circus formation, because seals have priorities.

Piper helped pass flames down the line, her competitive energy channeled into efficiency. “Three more! Come on, we can relight everyone before the next gust!”

Even the creatures who hadn’t spoken to Willa all season shuffled forward gratefully, and she greeted each one the same way — with a small candle, a steady flame, and the words: “There’s enough. Keep going.”

---

When the procession finally crested Starwatch Cliff, every single lantern was lit.

Dozens of warm golden flames held up against the vast dark sky, steady and bright, and the creatures of Frostpeak Valley stood together on the cliff’s edge and looked up.

And the sky *answered*.

The northern lights unfurled like silk — enormous curtains of green and violet and shimmering rose — rippling and folding across the heavens. At the same moment, shooting stars streaked between the colors, silver threads stitching the whole display together. The snow on the cliff turned emerald, then lavender, then back to white. The light danced on every face, every feather, every piece of fur.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Cleo whispered, “How did you know to bring enough for everybody?”

Willa watched the sky, her small face lit in aurora-green. “I didn’t know exactly what would happen,” she said softly. “I just planned for the part that usually does. Wind always comes. And it’s always good to have more light than you think you’ll need.”

There was another silence, filled with shooting stars.

Then Teddy and Tilda looked at each other with the grave seriousness of judges delivering a verdict.

Teddy pulled out a charcoal stub. Tilda held up her mitten.

With great ceremony, Teddy wrote on the mitten:

**PACK CANDLES.**

And underneath:

**MAYBE LESS MARSHMALLOWS.**

They stared at the second line.

They looked at each other.

They crossed it out immediately.

“We didn’t mean to write that—“ said Teddy.

“—but we’re glad we erased it,” said Tilda.

Willa laughed — a small, warm sound, barely louder than a candle flame — and they all sat together on the cliff as the sky blazed and shimmered above them, their lanterns pooling gold around their feet, the whole mountain glowing like a promise kept.
