## The Story

Nobody in Frostpeak Valley knew much about the snow-fox who lived at the top of Wallside Cliffs.

Her name was Raya, and she kept to herself. She had a small den carved into the ice near the cliff's edge, with a window that looked out over the entire valley. She could see the warming lodge and the cocoa cart and the tiny dots of penguins and bears sliding down the slopes, and she watched them the way you might watch a snow globe -- from the outside, nose pressed to the glass, not quite part of the picture.

Raya wasn't from Frostpeak Valley. She'd wandered in during a blizzard two winters ago, half-frozen and starving, and she'd found the empty den and stayed. She traded small ice carvings at the market for food and supplies, but she never joined the slope days or the cocoa circles or the campfire singalongs. She was polite. She was quiet. She was alone.

And then came the day the avalanche warning sounded.

It was a Thursday, cold and sharp, with a sky the color of steel wool. Bjorn Bigpaws had posted the warning flags at dawn -- three red triangles, which meant "stay off the upper cliffs until further notice." The snowpack above Wallside Cliffs was unstable, loaded with two weeks of powder sitting on a layer of hard crust. One wrong vibration and the whole shelf could break loose.

Everyone in the valley knew to stay low. Everyone, that is, except Piper Paddlefoot and Barnaby Beaksworth, who had gone up to the high cliffs early that morning -- before the flags went up -- to scout a new freestyle route.

Willa Wobble was the first to notice they were missing.

"Has anyone seen Piper?" she asked at the warming lodge, her small voice tight with worry. "She said she'd be back for breakfast. Barnaby too."

Finnegan checked the trail log. "They signed out for Upper Wallside at six a.m. That's above the avalanche line."

The room went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

Bjorn moved fast. "The main trail to Upper Wallside is blocked -- the snow shelf is hanging right over it. If we send a group up that way, we could trigger the slide ourselves and make everything worse."

"Is there another route?" Nora Snowmane asked, her calm voice barely hiding the urgency underneath.

Everyone looked at the map pinned to the lodge wall. There wasn't another route. Upper Wallside was bordered by cliffs on three sides and the unstable snowfield on the fourth. Piper and Barnaby were trapped on a ledge with no safe way down.

"There is one way," said a voice from the doorway.

Everyone turned. Raya Snow-Fox stood at the threshold, snow dusting her russet fur, her amber eyes bright and serious. She almost never came to the lodge. Most of the valley creatures had barely heard her speak.

"My den is at the top of Wallside," Raya said. "There's a narrow ice chimney behind it -- a vertical crack in the cliff. It's tight, and it's hidden, but it drops down to a ledge about fifty feet above the valley floor. If your friends can reach my den, I can lower them through the chimney with a rope."

"How would they reach your den?" Bjorn asked. "The snow shelf--"

"Doesn't cover the east face. There's a traverse -- icy, tricky, but passable if you're careful. I use it every time I come to market." Raya paused. "I can guide them across. But I need someone to go up with me."

Silence. The traverse Raya described sounded terrifying -- a narrow ice ledge with a sheer drop on one side and an unstable cliff on the other. Every sensible bone in every sensible body said to wait for the avalanche patrol from the next valley over.

Willa Wobble stepped forward. She was so small that she had to tilt her whole head back to look up at Raya. "I'll go. I'm light, and I'm steady, and Piper would come for me."

Raya looked at the little blue penguin -- barely bigger than a lunch box, wrapped in a scarf three times her size -- and something in her amber eyes softened.

"Then we go now," Raya said. "Before the wind picks up."

Bjorn handed Raya the longest rope in the equipment shed -- a bright scarlet ski rope, fifty meters of braided nylon, the kind used for cliff rescues. "Tie this to your den when you get there. We'll watch for it from below. When we see the scarlet rope hanging from the cliff, we'll know you've made it and we'll have the rescue sled ready."

Raya coiled the rope over her shoulder. Against her russet fur, it looked like a ribbon of red fire.

The climb was everything Raya had warned about and worse. The east traverse was a blade of ice barely wider than Willa's body, glazed with frost and tilted at an angle that made Willa's stomach do somersaults. Raya moved ahead, sure-footed, her claws finding grip where there seemed to be none, and she talked the entire time -- not because she liked talking, but because she could feel Willa trembling behind her.

"See that blue patch of ice? Skip it, step left. Good. Now the rock -- grab with both flippers. Perfect. You're a natural, Willa."

"I am NOT a natural," Willa panted. "I am a small penguin who is very, very determined."

"That's better than a natural," Raya said.

They reached the den in forty minutes. Raya's home was tiny but warm -- a hollow in the ice lined with moss and dried pine needles, with a small lantern and a stack of books and a window that framed the whole valley like a painting.

"It's beautiful up here," Willa whispered, momentarily forgetting to be scared.

"It's lonely up here," Raya corrected quietly. Then she shook herself. "Come on. Your friends are close."

They found Piper and Barnaby huddled on a ledge two hundred meters west, wrapped in each other's scarves, looking cold and sheepish and enormously relieved to see anyone at all.

"HOW did you get up here?" Piper sputtered.

"The snow-fox knows a way," Willa said simply.

Raya led all three of them back along the traverse to her den, moving slowly, calling out every handhold, every safe step. Barnaby kept up a steady stream of nervous chatter ("Is this ice or glass? Why is the sky so close? I should have packed more fish crackers for emergencies like this--"), and Piper was uncharacteristically quiet, her eyes wide, watching Raya's every move with growing admiration.

At the den, Raya tied the scarlet ski rope to the iron bolt she used for her market-day climbs. She fed the rope through the ice chimney -- a vertical crack just wide enough for a penguin or a fox -- and lowered it into the darkness.

"One at a time," she said. "I'll lower you. The rope is strong. Trust it."

Barnaby went first, because Piper insisted ("You've got the snacks -- the valley needs you more"), and he descended into the chimney with a yelp and a prayer and emerged at the bottom blinking in the daylight, where Bjorn caught him like a furry catcher's mitt.

Piper went next, spinning slowly on the scarlet rope, watching the ice walls of the chimney glitter around her like the inside of a geode.

Then Willa. Raya lowered her so gently that Willa barely felt the descent, just a slow, steady drop into the arms of her friends below.

From the valley floor, everyone looked up. The scarlet rope hung from the top of Wallside Cliffs like a bright red lifeline, vivid against the grey ice, and at the top, Raya's small russet face peered down.

"Come down!" Piper shouted up. "Please! There's cocoa!"

Raya hesitated. She'd lived alone for so long. The valley was so full of creatures who knew each other, who had histories and inside jokes and favorite seats at the warming lodge. She was just the fox on the cliff. The outsider.

"COME DOWN!" Willa's small voice echoed off the rocks. "You're one of us now!"

Raya rappelled down the scarlet rope, fast and graceful, and landed in the snow at the bottom to find herself immediately surrounded. Bjorn shook her paw solemnly. Finnegan tackle-hugged her (she was not prepared). Barnaby pressed a bag of fish crackers into her paws ("The good ones -- sea salt and rosemary"). The seal pups barked applause. Herschel poured her a cocoa with double marshmallows and a cinnamon stick, the kind he only gave to family.

"You saved them," Nora said warmly.

"Willa saved them," Raya said. "I just knew the way."

"That's what saving is," Willa said.

That evening, they sat in a circle around the warming lodge fire, and Raya sat with them -- really sat with them, not outside looking in, but right there in the middle, with Willa leaning against her side and Piper's scarf draped over her shoulders because "you look cold and I have seven scarves."

The scarlet rope still hung from the cliff, bright against the darkening sky. Bjorn said they should leave it there.

"As a reminder," he said. "That home isn't a place you're born into. It's a door someone opens for you -- and sometimes the bravest thing you can do is open it for someone else."

Raya looked around the circle at the faces glowing in the firelight -- these ridiculous, wonderful, cracker-eating, avalanche-surviving creatures -- and for the first time in two years, she didn't feel like she was watching a snow globe from the outside.

She was inside it.

And it was warm.
