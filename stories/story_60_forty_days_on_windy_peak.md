**Theme:** Choosing what is right

## The Story

**Theo Tundra-Wolf** had decided to spend forty days on Windy Peak alone.

Not because he was in trouble, and not because he was running away from anything. Theo was a large, grey-and-silver wolf who had been skiing competitively in Frostpeak Valley for three seasons, and he had a championship qualifier coming in the spring, and he had decided that the best preparation was extended solo training on the mountain's most demanding terrain. Forty days of wind, cold, hard practice, and nobody to compare himself to but himself.

Everyone thought this was a bold plan. Willa Wobble thought it was smart training methodology. Finnegan thought it was the most dramatic thing anyone had ever done and said so loudly and with great admiration.

"I'll be here when you get back," said **Willa Wobble**, handing him a packed sled at the trailhead.

"I know," said Theo. "Thank you."

He went.

---

The first temptation came on day nine.

Theo was cold and tired — the kind of tired that comes from eleven days of solo camping and hard training without rest days — and his food supply was lower than he'd planned because the cold was burning more calories than expected. He was sitting outside his tent eating his careful dinner ration when a voice spoke at the edge of the firelight.

It was a lean, clever-looking fox with yellow eyes. He appeared from nowhere, which should have been a warning, and sat just outside the fire's warmth, which should have been a second one.

"Rough conditions up here," said the fox.

"Yes," said Theo.

"You know, the supply cache at the mid-mountain depot is unlocked," said the fox. "Emergency supplies. Not monitored this time of year. You could eat well tonight and nobody would know."

Theo looked at the fox. He looked at his own dinner. It was enough — just enough, measured carefully. The cache was for genuine emergencies.

"I have enough," said Theo. "This is enough."

The fox's yellow eyes were steady. "Enough isn't always comfortable."

"No," said Theo. "But comfortable isn't the point."

The fox went away.

---

The second temptation came on day twenty-three.

Theo was at the summit ridge, training the steepest technical section, and he had fallen twice on the same turn. His form was tired and he knew it. A shadow came across the snow and spoke in a tone that was reasonable and very slightly persuasive.

"You know," said the shadow — it seemed to have the shape of a wolf, but the wrong kind — "if you skip the qualifier's technical scoring section and focus only on speed, you'd almost certainly place. Most judges don't watch the technicalities as closely as the overall time."

Theo stood on his skis at the top of the turn. He looked at the fall line.

"I'm not here to almost win," he said. "I'm here to ski it right."

"Right is harder."

"Yes," said Theo. "That's what practice is for." He pushed off and ran the technical section again, and fell again on the same turn, and got up, and ran it once more.

On the fourth try it was clean.

The shadow was gone.

---

The third temptation came on day thirty-nine.

Theo was almost done. He was tired in the deep way that forty days of wind and solo work creates — tired in his bones, in his paws, in the quiet corners of his mind. And the voice this time was gentler and more specific.

"You've worked so hard," said the voice. "You deserve recognition. Tell the valley what you've done here. Tell them you're already better than everyone who stayed home. Make them impressed before you even get back."

Theo thought about Willa at the trailhead, handing him the sled, saying *I'll be here when you get back*. He thought about what it would feel like to ski the qualifier well — not to be told he was better, but to actually *be* better, in the moment, on the mountain, which was the only place it was real.

"The mountain knows what I did here," said Theo. "That's enough."

He broke camp on day forty and skied home.

---

**Willa** was at the trailhead. She had brought cocoa, because she was Willa.

Theo came down the last section and stopped and stood in the snow looking at the valley spread below him — all the familiar runs, the lights of the Warming Hut in the distance, the ordinary beautiful sight of home.

"How was it?" Willa asked.

"Hard," said Theo. "Very windy. Three times I almost made the wrong choice."

Willa handed him the cocoa. "But you didn't."

"No."

"Then the hard part was worth it."

Theo wrapped his cold paws around the mug. He was tired in a way that felt clean now — the right kind of tired, the kind that comes from going through something rather than around it.

"I got the technical section on the fourth try," he said. "On day thirty-nine."

"Show me," said Willa. "Not today. But soon."

"Deal," said Theo.

The qualifier was in spring. He was going to be ready.
