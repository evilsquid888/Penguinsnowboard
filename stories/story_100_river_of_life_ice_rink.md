# Story 100: River of Life Ice Rink
### *Inspired by River of Life — Revelation 22*
**Characters:** Riv Robin, Cleo Coldwater  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Crystal River Rink  
**Theme:** Healing joy

## The Story

**Cleo Coldwater** had been painting the Crystal River for three winters, and she still hadn't gotten it right.

Not wrong, exactly — the paintings were good, creatures asked about them, Barnaby had one hung in the warming hut. But there was something about the Crystal River Rink that kept exceeding what she put on paper. The ice there formed differently than on the built rinks — slower, in the cold quiet of the natural river, and clearer. You could see four feet down through it on a still morning, the river suspended beneath, dark water and white silt and the occasional astonished fish peering up at the sky.

On a Tuesday morning in late winter, she was setting up her paints at the river's edge when she heard footsteps in the snow behind her.

She looked up.

**Riv Robin** was standing at the bank, looking at the ice.

---

Riv Robin was a small, round-chested robin who had skated the Crystal River Rink every winter for as long as anyone could remember — until three winters ago, when a bad fall on the competition rink had cracked a wing bone. The bone had healed. But the wing had healed slightly differently, and balance was not what it had been, and the competition rink was loud and fast and required a kind of confidence Riv hadn't quite found her way back to.

She had watched from the sidelines for three winters. She watched others skate with something that was not quite envy — more like grief, the way you feel about something you loved and are not sure you can return to.

She had not come to the Crystal River Rink in all that time.

Cleo said nothing. She watched Riv look at the ice.

The ice was doing what it did on still mornings — holding everything still, reflecting the pale early light, showing the dark river beneath. A single track crossed it from the night before, some small creature who had gone over in the dark.

"Is it safe?" Riv asked.

"Bjorn checked it yesterday," said Cleo. "It's good. Twelve centimeters, no soft spots."

Riv was quiet.

"I haven't skated in three winters," she said.

"I know," said Cleo.

"My balance is different now."

"Probably," said Cleo. "The ice doesn't know that."

---

Riv sat on the bank and laced her skates. Her hands remembered the motion even when the rest of her felt uncertain. She had laced these skates hundreds of times.

She stood at the edge of the ice and looked at it for a long moment.

Then she stepped on.

The first three seconds were very unsteady. Her left side — the side of the old injury — took a moment to find where it was. She put a wing out for balance. She did not fall.

The fourth second was better.

She took a push, cautious. Then another. The ice was smooth and slow in the early-morning cold, not like the competition rink's bright hard surface — softer, somehow, more patient. She went ten meters without urgency and found that her legs remembered what her mind had been worried about.

Not the same as before. But not gone.

She went further. The river stretched ahead of her, wide and quiet, bordered by snow-laden pines. The only sounds were her blades and the occasional creak of the ice contracting in the morning cold and, faintly, Cleo's brush moving somewhere behind her on the bank.

She skated.

---

An hour later, she came back to the bank and sat beside Cleo.

Cleo was painting — not Riv, which Riv appreciated. She was painting the river itself: the dark water visible below the ice, the pine shadows, the single track from the night creature.

"It's different," said Riv. "Skating. It's different than it was."

"Yes," said Cleo.

"But it's still there."

"Yes," said Cleo. "The river's still there. The ice is still there. You're still a skater." She paused, considering the painting. "Just a different one than before."

Riv looked at the river beneath the ice — the dark water, the suspended motion, the fish somewhere below in the cold. There was something about the Crystal River that had always felt like this: like something was moving underneath, even when the surface was still.

"Why did you come here to paint?" Riv asked. "You've been here every Tuesday."

"Because I can't get it right," said Cleo. "Every time I paint it, it's better than I got. So I come back."

"What are you trying to get?"

Cleo looked at the ice for a moment.

"The thing under the surface," she said. "The way the river's still moving even when it's frozen. You can see it if you look." She tapped the paper. "But I haven't found the way to show it yet."

---

They sat together in the quiet while Cleo painted and the morning warmed by degrees.

Other creatures began to arrive — the Crystal River Rink was not a secret, but it was not crowded, the way the built rinks were crowded. Wida Wren came at mid-morning and skated her slow careful loops. An otter family arrived and slid rather than skated, which was their preferred method and looked extremely satisfying. Barnaby appeared at noon with a thermos, which he always did on Tuesdays, because Barnaby had determined that Tuesday mornings at the Crystal River Rink were the kind of thing that benefited from a thermos appearing at noon.

Riv skated again in the afternoon — longer this time, and with less worry in it. Her balance was different but it was *hers*, and the river was patient and the ice was clear and she had been away for three winters and was back now.

At some point she realized she was not grieving the old version of herself. She was just skating.

Cleo, watching from the bank between brushstrokes, made a note in the margin of her sketchbook: *the river doesn't stop for the ice. it just waits.*

---

At the end of the afternoon, when the light was going gold and the aurora was beginning its slow stir at the horizon, Riv sat beside Cleo and looked at the painting.

Cleo had gotten closer today than she had before. The dark water under the ice was there, somehow — not painted as water exactly, but as a quality of depth, something moving beneath the still surface.

"That's it," said Riv.

"Almost," said Cleo.

"What's missing?"

Cleo considered. "I think you have to be here to see it," she said. "That's what I keep trying to put in. You have to be standing at the edge and then come on anyway."

Riv looked at the river. She thought about three winters of watching from the sidelines, and the morning on the ice, and the way the balance was different but the skating was still there.

"Healing joy," she said. Not to Cleo exactly — more to herself, or to the river.

"Is that what it is?" said Cleo.

"I think so. Not fixed. Not like before. Just — joy that's come through something." She paused. "The river kept going under the ice the whole time I wasn't skating."

"It did," said Cleo. "It always does."

The aurora was fully up now — the full spread of rose and green and that particular blue-white that only came at the height of the winter sky, when the light was doing everything it knew how to do. It reflected off the Crystal River ice in long wavering bands, and the dark water beneath the surface caught it too, dimly, as if the river remembered being open and was showing what it could reach of the light.

Sixty creatures, and then eighty, came to the Crystal River Rink that evening — word had traveled that the ice was perfect and the aurora was extraordinary and Barnaby had a second thermos, and this was all anyone needed. They skated until the cold deepened and the stars appeared above the aurora and the river ice glowed with everything it was reflecting.

Healing joy is the kind that doesn't erase what was hard. It holds what was hard inside it, the way the river holds its motion under the ice — still there, still moving, even when you couldn't see it. And one morning you come to the bank and lace your skates and step on anyway, and the river is glad you came, and the ice holds you, and the joy that was waiting is still waiting, patient as frozen water over a living river.

It was always going to be here. It just needed you to come back.
