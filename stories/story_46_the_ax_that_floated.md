# Story 46: The Axe That Floated
### *Inspired by Floating Axe Head — 2 Kings 6*
**Characters:** Axel Beaver, Piper Paddlefoot  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Workshop Pond  
**Theme:** God cares about little losses

---

## Opening Wink

Here's a secret: the smallest lost things sometimes get the most miraculous finding.

## The Story

The trouble started because Finnegan Flipsworth wanted to build a birdhouse.

Not just any birdhouse. A birdhouse shaped like a snowboard, with a tiny front porch shaped like a ski, and a chimney shaped like a fish cracker. "It'll be the fanciest birdhouse in the whole valley!" Finnegan declared, bouncing from foot to foot with the kind of energy that made everyone around him both excited and slightly nervous.

There was only one problem. Finnegan did not own a snow-saw.

A snow-saw was a special tool used in Frostpeak Valley for cutting blocks of packed snow and ice into building shapes. They were beautifully made—carved wooden handles, bright steel blades with frost-resistant edges—and they were not cheap. Finnegan had spent all his savings on a set of glow-in-the-dark ski wax that turned out to just be regular wax with glitter in it.

"You can borrow mine," said Piper Paddlefoot, handing over her snow-saw without hesitation. It was a beauty—maple handle, blade sharp enough to cut clean lines through packed powder, with Piper's initials carved into the grip. "Just be careful with it. My grandmother gave it to me."

"Careful is my middle name!" Finnegan announced.

"Your middle name is Danger," Barnaby Beaksworth pointed out, not looking up from his fish crackers.

"Careful Danger Flipsworth," Finnegan amended. "I'll guard it with my life."

He tucked the snow-saw into his belt and waddled off to Workshop Pond, a wide, flat area near the frozen creek where everyone in the valley came to build things. The snow there was packed firm from years of use, perfect for cutting. Stumps served as workbenches. A row of lanterns hung from a rope strung between two birch trees, casting wobbling circles of gold light across the workspace.

Cleo Coldwater was already there, sculpting a snow statue of a narwhal playing a violin. Willa Wobble sat nearby, carefully measuring a snow-brick with a tiny ruler. The Powderpuff twins were attempting to build a snow fort, though they seemed to be mostly throwing snow at each other and calling it "structural testing."

Finnegan set to work. He drew his birdhouse design in the snow with a stick, muttering measurements that were mostly wrong ("Twelve inches... or maybe twelve feet... same difference..."). He picked up Piper's snow-saw and began cutting his first block.

The work was satisfying—the saw bit into the packed snow with a clean whisper, and curls of white powder fell away like wood shavings. Finnegan hummed as he worked, rocking the saw back and forth with increasing enthusiasm.

Too much enthusiasm, as it turned out.

On a particularly vigorous stroke, the saw jumped out of the groove, flew out of Finnegan's flippers, arced through the lantern light in a graceful, spinning trajectory, and landed—*plunk*—right in the middle of Workshop Pond.

Workshop Pond was not frozen solid. The center was a deep, dark circle of open water, cold and clear, and Piper's grandmother's snow-saw sank into it like a stone dropping into ink.

Finnegan stared at the spot where the saw had vanished. A single bubble rose to the surface and popped.

"Oh no," he whispered. "Oh no, oh no, oh NO."

Cleo looked up from her narwhal. Willa set down her ruler. Even the Powderpuff twins stopped their structural testing.

"Was that Piper's saw?" Willa asked gently.

Finnegan's crest drooped flat against his head. His lower beak wobbled. "It was borrowed," he said miserably. "It was her grandmother's. And I lost it. I lost it in the pond."

He sat down on a stump and put his head in his flippers. "She trusted me and I lost it. I can't buy a new one—it was special. It was *hers*."

"Maybe we can fish it out!" Teddy Powderpuff suggested. He grabbed a long stick and poked it into the pond. The stick was about three feet too short. "Never mind."

"We could drain the pond," Tilda offered.

"We absolutely cannot drain the pond," Willa said firmly.

Finnegan looked up. His eyes were wet, and not from the cold. "I have to tell Piper," he said. "I have to tell her the truth."

This, everyone agreed, was the right thing to do. But it was also the hardest thing, which is usually how right things work.

They found Piper at the main lodge, waxing her snowboard by the fire. She looked up with a grin when she saw Finnegan, and the grin faded when she saw his face.

"What happened?" she asked.

"I lost your snow-saw," Finnegan said. All in one breath, no excuses, no funny stories to soften it. "I was cutting too fast and it flew out of my flippers and went into Workshop Pond. Into the deep part. I'm so sorry, Piper. I know it was your grandmother's. I know I can't replace it."

Piper was quiet for a moment. The fire crackled. A log shifted and sent a little fountain of sparks up the chimney.

"Show me," she said.

They all walked back to Workshop Pond together—Piper, Finnegan, Willa, Cleo, Barnaby (who had joined the group because he smelled the possibility of a story worth snacking through), and the twins. The moon was high now, and the pond shone like a dark mirror rimmed with frost lace.

Piper stood at the edge and looked into the water. It was deep and black and still. The saw was nowhere to be seen.

"I know you didn't mean to," Piper said quietly. "I could tell the second I saw your face. You came and told me the truth instead of hiding it, and that matters more than any tool."

"But it was your grandmother's—"

"My grandmother," Piper said, "would have said that honesty is worth more than a hundred snow-saws."

She knelt at the edge of the pond, and—though nobody could explain what happened next, not then and not later—she placed one flipper gently on the surface of the water. Not reaching in. Just touching it, the way you might rest your hand on a friend's shoulder.

The water shivered. A ripple spread outward from her flipper in a perfect circle, silver in the moonlight. Then another ripple, coming back inward, as if the pond were breathing. The dark surface began to glow—faintly at first, then brighter, a soft blue-white light rising from the deep.

And then, slowly, impossibly, the snow-saw floated to the surface.

It rose through the water like a leaf falling upward, turning gently, the maple handle catching the moonlight. It broke the surface without a splash and bobbed there, steady as a little boat, right in front of Piper's flipper.

Nobody breathed.

Piper lifted the saw from the water. It was dry. It was warm. The blade gleamed as if it had just been polished, and her grandmother's initials on the handle seemed to glow faintly, like embers.

"How..." Finnegan started.

"I don't know," Piper said. She was smiling—not her usual wild, full-throttle grin, but something softer. Something wondering. "I think... I think sometimes when you tell the truth about a little thing you lost, something bigger than you helps you find it again."

Willa nodded slowly, as if this was a piece of wisdom she'd always known but had been waiting to see proved. "Small losses matter," she said. "Every single one."

Finnegan looked at the saw, then at Piper, then at the pond, which had gone still and dark again as if nothing had happened. "I promise I'll never borrow anything without being triple careful again," he said.

"Double careful is fine," Piper said. "Triple careful would slow you down too much, and then you'd never build anything."

Finnegan laughed—a watery, relieved, grateful laugh. Barnaby handed him a fish cracker, which is the chinstrap penguin equivalent of a hug.

Later that night, back at the workshop, Finnegan finished his birdhouse. It was lopsided. The porch was crooked. The chimney looked more like a pretzel than a fish cracker. But he built it with borrowed tools and honest flippers, and when Piper saw it, she said it was the most beautiful birdhouse in the whole valley.

She was probably exaggerating. But Finnegan hung it in the birch tree by the workshop anyway, and by morning, a tiny snow bunting had moved in and was singing from the crooked porch like it was a palace.

And Piper's snow-saw sat safe on its hook in the lodge, dry and warm and gleaming, as if it had never been lost at all—or as if being lost and found again had made it just a little bit more precious than before.
