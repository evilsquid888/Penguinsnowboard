# Story 24: Jacob's Icy Ladder Dream
### *Inspired by Jacob's Ladder — Genesis 28:10–22*
**Characters:** Finnegan Flipsworth, Whistlewick  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, North Pass  
**Theme:** God is near in lonely places

---

## Opening Wink

*Revision Pass 3 Spark:* When Finnegan woke up, he could still feel the warmth of a very large and very kind presence, like a blanket that had covered the whole mountain.

Tonight's forecast: 30% snowfall, 70% wonder, and a 100% chance that the loneliest place in the valley turns out to be the most watched-over. Finnegan Flipsworth did not know this when he fell asleep in the snow. He found out the interesting way.

## The Story

**Finnegan Flipsworth** had been running away from something.

He hadn't meant to. He'd just started heading north after the argument — it was a small argument, the kind that feels enormous when you're in the middle of it — and kept walking past the Warming Hut, past the practice slope, past the first marker post on the North Pass trail, and just... kept going. His red snowboard was strapped to his back and his crest was bouncing and his feet were moving and his brain was doing the specific thing it did when he was upset: generating excuses, explanations, and alternative histories in which he had not said the wrong thing.

"I didn't mean it that way," he told the trees.

The trees did not respond.

"I just — it came out wrong. That happens. Things come out wrong." He kicked a snow clump. "I *always* say the wrong thing."

The path climbed and narrowed. The valley lights fell away behind him. Around him, the forest went from familiar to unfamiliar to somewhere he'd technically never been, which Finnegan only noticed about thirty minutes in when the trail markers stopped and the trees started looking at him funny.

He stopped.

He looked around.

The path had... diverged. Somewhere. Into approximately four equally plausible options, all heading in different directions, none of them marked.

"Right," said Finnegan. "Okay. This is fine. This is a classic Finnegan situation, and I handle these all the time."

He did not, statistically, handle them that well.

He picked a direction and walked for ten minutes and found himself in a hollow he didn't recognize, ringed by ice-heavy pines, with the sky going from blue-grey to deep navy above him. The temperature had dropped a tooth-chattering degree. His breath made clouds.

He tried to backtrack and couldn't find the path he'd come in on.

"WOOOHOOOOO—" he started, then stopped himself because he was alone and it seemed wrong to be enthusiastic about being lost.

He sat down in the snow against a large flat rock. He pulled his snowboard around for warmth. He wrapped his scarf approximately four times around his head, which helped.

The thing about being alone in the dark, away from everything, is that it gives you nowhere to look except inward, and inward was where the argument still sat, sharp-edged and uncomfortable. He thought about what he'd said. He thought about the face that had looked back at him. He thought about all the times words had flown out of his beak before his brain had checked them over.

The sky above the pines went fully dark. Stars appeared — more than he usually noticed, this far from the valley lights. The cold bit at his nose.

Finnegan Flipsworth — who was always loud, always moving, always arriving places like a small and enthusiastic explosion — felt very small.

*You are not as alone as you feel*, said something.

Not in words exactly. More like the feeling you get when someone puts a warm blanket around your shoulders — the sense of something present and large and completely, specifically concerned with you.

Finnegan went still.

He hadn't heard it. He'd *felt* it. The way you feel warmth from a fire before you see the flame.

He looked at the sky. The aurora had appeared — green and quiet, barely more than a suggestion, threading between the stars. And above it, impossibly, he seemed to see — or imagine, or half-dream in the cold and the loneliness — a brightness, like something enormous and warm and high up, its light reaching all the way down to where he was.

Like a ladder of light from the sky to the snow.

*You are here. And I am here. And you are not lost in any way that matters.*

---

Finnegan fell asleep sitting up against the flat rock, which was neither planned nor comfortable, and woke up with the first grey light of dawn showing through the pines.

**Whistlewick the Arctic Fox** was standing three feet away, bright eyes watching him with the patient alertness of a creature who had been there a while.

"Finnegan Flipsworth," Whistlewick said, "you have been asleep in the snow for several hours, which is impressive even by Frostpeak standards."

"I got lost," Finnegan said.

"I know. I've been watching the trail. When you didn't come back, I came looking." Whistlewick tilted his head. "Are you all right?"

Finnegan thought about the feeling — the warmth, the light, the presence that had made the cold feel less cold and the alone feel less alone.

"Yes," he said, and meant it in a way he didn't quite have words for.

"You should have left a note," Whistlewick said, which was very Whistlewick.

"I know. I'll leave a note next time."

"Or you could just not run off into North Pass after an argument."

Finnegan looked at the scout fox. "I said the wrong thing. To Bjorn. I said the wrong thing and I felt bad about it and I walked here instead of fixing it."

Whistlewick said nothing for a moment. Then: "That's a long way to walk for a problem that has a shorter solution."

"Yeah."

"Bjorn is fine, by the way. He wants to talk to you. He sent me to find you."

Finnegan blinked. "He *sent* you?"

"Said to tell you: 'What you said isn't why I'm worried. The leaving is.'"

The cold air suddenly felt warmer. Finnegan pulled himself to his feet, shook snow off his coat, and looked back through the pines at the trail home.

"I had the strangest dream," he said. "Or I saw something. Or felt something. I don't know how to explain it."

Whistlewick looked at him calmly. "Maybe you don't have to explain it. Maybe you just get to carry it."

Finnegan adjusted his snowboard. He looked at the flat rock where he'd slept, ordinary and grey in the morning light, with no sign of ladders or light or warm presences — just stone and snow and pine needles.

He touched it once, lightly, with one flipper.

"Thank you," he said quietly. He wasn't entirely sure who he was talking to.

Then he turned toward home.

"WOOOHOOOOO—wait, which way—"

"*Left*," said Whistlewick.

"Left! Perfect! I knew that!"

He didn't, but that was fine. Whistlewick knew, and Whistlewick was here, and the valley was that way, and home was that way, and the ladder of light had shown him — even in the loneliest, coldest place on the mountain — that he had never really been alone at all.
