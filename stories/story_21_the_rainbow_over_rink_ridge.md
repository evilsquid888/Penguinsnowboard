# Story 21: The Rainbow Over Rink Ridge
### *Inspired by God's Promise After the Flood — Genesis 9:8–17*
**Characters:** Piper Paddlefoot, Cleo Coldwater, Captain Prism  
**Setting:** Frostpeak Valley, Frozen Mirror Lake Rim  
**Theme:** Promise-keeping and hope after storms

---

## Opening Wink

After the worst storms, the sky sometimes does something so beautiful it makes even grumpy walruses cry. This is a story about what happened the morning after the biggest blizzard Frostpeak Valley had ever seen.

## The Story

The Great Storm of Last Thursday -- which Herschel the Walrus would later call "the rudest weather I have experienced in forty-seven years of sitting on things" -- had lasted three full days.

Three days of wind that sounded like a freight train arguing with a tuba. Three days of snow so thick you couldn't see your own flippers. Three days of everyone crammed into the Main Lodge, eating emergency rations (fish crackers, cocoa, and whatever Barnaby Beaksworth found in the back of the pantry, which turned out to be a surprising amount of marshmallows and one very old pickle).

But now it was over. The wind had stopped. The snow had settled. And when Piper Paddlefoot pushed open the lodge door on Friday morning, the silence that greeted her was so enormous it felt like the whole valley was holding its breath.

"It's... done?" she whispered.

Behind her, Cleo Coldwater peered over Piper's shoulder, her macaroni penguin crest still rumpled from sleeping on a pile of coats. "Is the sky still there?"

"The sky is still there," Piper confirmed.

They stepped outside together. The valley was transformed. Snowdrifts had piled up in shapes that looked like frozen ocean waves, some taller than Bjorn Bigpaws. The runs were buried. Rink Ridge, the smooth natural halfpipe that curved along the edge of Frozen Mirror Lake, was completely hidden under fresh powder. The old trail markers were gone. The flagpoles wore snow hats. Even the Cocoa Station chimney had a drift leaning against it like a sleepy white bear.

"It looks like a different place," Cleo said quietly. She pulled out her sketchbook, which she kept in her vest at all times because "you never know when beauty will happen," and began to draw.

Piper stepped down from the lodge porch. The snow crunched under her feet with that particular sound -- half squeak, half whisper -- that only comes from perfectly fresh powder. She looked left. She looked right. She looked at the sky, which was a pale, tired gray, like it had been up all night and hadn't had its coffee yet.

Something heavy sat in her chest. It wasn't fear exactly. It was more like... the worry that comes after something big and scary stops, when you're still bracing for it to start again.

"What if it comes back?" she said.

Cleo stopped drawing. "The storm?"

"A bigger one. What if the next one is worse? What if the valley can't take it?" Piper's flippers were fidgeting, the way they always did when her brain was running faster than her feet. "What if we just... keep getting hit, over and over, and one day the lodge doesn't hold and the cocoa runs out and the --"

"Piper." Cleo put a flipper on her shoulder. "Breathe."

Piper breathed. The cold air tasted like pine needles and clean snow and the faintest hint of Herschel's cocoa drifting out the lodge chimney.

"I'm breathing," she said. "But I'm still worried."

By midmorning, the valley was awake and everyone was worried, too. Bjorn had organized a damage assessment team (clipboard included), and the news was mostly good -- structures intact, no one hurt, supplies holding -- but the *mood* was fragile. Animals stood in doorways and looked at the sky the way you look at someone who just knocked over your bookshelf and is standing there pretending nothing happened.

"Will it storm again tonight?" Teddy Powderpuff asked, hugging his sister Tilda's arm.

"Nobody knows," Nora Snowmane said gently. "But we are here together, and that is the important part."

"The *important* part is more marshmallows," Tilda said firmly.

"Also that," Nora agreed.

The valley spent the day digging out. Shovels scraped. Snow flew. Willa Wobble organized a supply chain of hot drinks from the lodge to the work crews, because Willa understood that morale ran on cocoa. Barnaby discovered that his entire fish cracker stash had survived the storm untouched, which he declared "a miracle of snack engineering."

But the sky stayed gray. And people kept glancing up.

It was late afternoon when Captain Prism arrived.

He came from the direction of Frozen Mirror Lake, which was his preferred entrance because the ice there reflected light in a way that made his horn sparkle, and Captain Prism was not an animal who wasted sparkle opportunities. He was a narwhal of considerable size, deep blue-gray with a long spiral horn that caught every stray photon in a ten-mile radius. He wore a cape. Nobody had asked him to wear a cape. He wore one anyway.

"CITIZENS OF FROSTPEAK!" he announced, surfacing through a break in the lake ice with a spray of glittering water. "I BRING NEWS FROM THE FAR RIDGE."

"You're very loud," Willa observed.

"Thank you," Captain Prism said, choosing to take this as a compliment. "I have just come from the far side of Rink Ridge, where I witnessed something that I believe will interest every soul in this valley." He paused dramatically. He was very good at pausing dramatically. "But first -- has anyone got cocoa? My journey was harrowing."

Herschel personally delivered a mug the size of a soup bowl. Captain Prism sipped, closed his eyes, and nodded gravely. "Acceptable. Now -- everyone to Rink Ridge. Immediately."

"Why?" Piper asked.

"Because," Captain Prism said, "the sky is about to make a promise."

Nobody fully understood what that meant, but when a narwhal in a cape tells you to follow him, you follow him. The whole valley trekked up the freshly buried slope to the rim of Rink Ridge, where the powder was so deep that Willa, being a Little Blue Penguin, basically disappeared into it and had to ride on Bjorn's shoulder.

"I feel like a parrot," she said.

"You are too dignified to be a parrot," Bjorn assured her.

They reached the ridge just as the sun found a crack in the clouds.

It wasn't a big crack. Just a sliver, low on the western horizon, where the gray ceiling of sky pulled apart like a curtain being drawn back by an invisible hand. Golden light poured through -- thick, warm, honeyed light that turned the snow from white to amber and made every ice crystal on every surface ignite like a tiny star.

"Oh," Cleo said, her sketchbook already out.

And then the rainbow came.

It didn't appear all at once. It grew. First a shimmer of red, arcing up from the far end of Rink Ridge. Then orange, layering beneath it like a brushstroke. Then yellow, green, blue -- each band materializing as if someone were painting it in real time across the enormous gray canvas of the post-storm sky.

The rainbow stretched from one side of the valley to the other, its feet planted on the ridgelines like a bridge made of light. Below it, Frozen Mirror Lake did what it did best: it mirrored. The reflection was so perfect that the rainbow seemed to form a complete circle, the real arch above and the reflected arch below, meeting at the edges of the ice.

Nobody spoke.

For a full thirty seconds, the only sounds were the wind settling, the soft creak of ice, and somewhere far below, a single seal barking in what sounded like amazement.

Captain Prism stepped forward. His horn caught the rainbow light and scattered it in tiny prismatic sparkles across the snow. He did not say anything dramatic. For once, he was quiet, and the quiet was more powerful than any monologue.

It was Piper who spoke first.

"It's a promise," she whispered. She didn't know why she said it. The words just came, like the rainbow had, out of something bigger than herself.

Cleo was crying. Not sad tears -- the kind that come when you see something so beautiful that your heart fills up past its edges and has to let some out. She was also drawing furiously, trying to capture every color before it faded.

"A promise of what?" Teddy asked.

Piper thought about it. She thought about the three days of wind, and the worry, and the gray sky, and standing in the doorway that morning afraid it would never stop. And she thought about this -- this impossible, shimmering, arching light that had come out of the storm's own clouds, as if the sky were saying: *I know. I know it was hard. But look. Look what comes after.*

"A promise that storms end," Piper said. "A promise that the sky comes back. A promise that..." She trailed off.

"That the hard part is not the whole story," Willa finished quietly, from Bjorn's shoulder.

Captain Prism cleared his throat. "I would like to add," he said, with as much restraint as he was capable of, "that the color palette is *exquisite*. Note the violet gradient near the apex. Truly museum-quality meteorology."

"You're ruining the moment," Cleo said, still drawing.

"I am *enhancing* the moment with informed commentary," Captain Prism said with dignity.

The rainbow held for almost ten minutes. In that time, animals who had spent the day anxious and storm-shaken stood together and watched the sky keep its promise. Nora put her arms around Teddy and Tilda. Bjorn stood steady with Willa on his shoulder. Barnaby distributed fish crackers, because Barnaby distributed fish crackers at every significant life event. Herschel cried openly into his cocoa mug and pretended he wasn't.

When the colors finally began to fade, Piper turned to Cleo. "Did you get it?"

Cleo held up her sketchbook. She had drawn the rainbow, the lake, the ridge, and every single friend standing together in the snow, small and bundled and looking up.

"I got it," she said. "I got all of it."

That night, back in the lodge, the mood was different. Not because the worry was completely gone -- you can't erase a three-day storm from your memory with one rainbow, no matter how spectacular. But something had shifted. The sky had cracked open and poured color into a gray world, and everyone had seen it together, and that togetherness was its own kind of promise.

Piper sat by the fire, wrapped in two blankets and holding the biggest mug of cocoa Herschel had ever poured.

"Do you think it'll storm again?" Teddy asked, already half asleep in Nora's lap.

Piper thought about this honestly. "Probably," she said. "Sometime."

"But then what?"

Piper smiled. "Then we wait. And we watch. And the sky will show us something beautiful on the other side."

Captain Prism, who had wedged himself into the lodge doorway because narwhals do not fit inside lodges but *do* refuse to miss important conversations, added: "And the colors will be *magnificent*."

"Goodnight, Captain," everyone said.

"Goodnight, citizens," he replied. "Sleep well. The sky is keeping watch."

And it was. And it did. And outside, the snow settled soft and quiet under stars that blinked like promises -- tiny, bright, and too many to count.
